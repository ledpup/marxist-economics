# Money and labour certificates in the Spanish Revolution

Did the Spanish anarchists abolish money in 1936–39, or did they make a new one? Did they, or anyone else in the Republican camp, propose anything like Marx's labour certificates? This note answers from Sam Dolgoff's collection [*The Anarchist Collectives*](../Documents/The_Anarchist_Collectives-money.md) (1974) and from the CNT's own programme. It compares both with the GIC's *Fundamental Principles*, and ends with a table of the factions in the war.

This is a research note by Claude (Opus 5.5), written from a brief by Patrick. Points marked *my reading* are interpretation, not something the sources say. Page numbers for *The Anarchist Collectives* are the book's printed pages; the [local PDF](../PDFs/THE%20ANARCHIST%20COLLECTIVES.pdf) is an OCR scan.

## Summary

- **Both, and mostly the second.** Many collectives stopped using the peseta inside the village. In its place they issued their own local money: vouchers, coupons, tokens, ration booklets, "certificates". A few, such as Magdalena de Pulpis, used no tokens at all. But the peseta stayed the unit of account almost everywhere. Dolgoff's own conclusion: "abolishing money" meant abolishing the *national* money for local use, not abolishing money ([Money and Exchange](../Documents/The_Anarchist_Collectives-money.md#money-and-exchange), p. 73).
- **Distribution was by need, not by labour time.** The usual system was the "family wage": a payment, in pesetas or local scrip, that rose with the size of the family. It was "based on the needs of the family rather than on the product of the family members" (p. 74). That is the opposite principle to Marx's certificate.
- **The CNT's programme did propose a labour certificate.** The Zaragoza Congress of May 1936, two months before the war, adopted a "producer's card" (*carta de productor*). It was to be non-transferable, to record "the value of the work … in units of a working day", and to lapse after a year at most. Neither Dolgoff nor Bookchin mentions it. This is the nearest thing to Marx's certificate in the Spanish movement.
- **It was not carried out as written.** The closest practice found is Alcora, where a family's card was punched each day at work and each day's work earned coupons worth five pesetas (pp. 143–144). The unit there was the peseta, not the working day, and the card belonged to the family.
- **The GIC's book quotes the CNT of 1931, not 1936.** Its CNT section ([ch. 5 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/05_Libertarian_Communism.md#e-anarcho-syndicalism)) works from reports of the June 1931 congress and complains: "On what economic bases does consumption take place? … We hear nothing about it!" The 1936 producer's card is a partial answer that the book could not have known about. The council communists' verdicts on Spain in 1937 were harsh: money remained and "the wage system is upheld".
- **Bookchin adds almost nothing on money.** Patrick's guess is right. His introduction mentions it once, in passing (p. xxxix). His *The Spanish Anarchists* (1977) stops in 1936.
- **The UGT never proposed certificates, but it did have a line on pay: by work done, in pesetas.** Its land federation ran over a quarter of the collectives. By secondary accounts, its collectives tended to pay each member by work and output, not by family size. In March 1938 the CNT signed a joint programme with the UGT on the UGT's terms: a minimum wage graded by skill and output, on the principle "more and better production, more pay" ([below](#the-ugt)).
- **No other faction found said anything on certificates.** Not the POUM, the FAI as a body, or the Communists. The Friends of Durruti (1938) asked for a family wage and rationing.

## The book

*The Anarchist Collectives: Workers' Self-management in the Spanish Revolution (1936-1939)*, edited by Sam Dolgoff, introductory essay by Murray Bookchin (New York: Free Life Editions, 1974). Dolgoff (1902–1990) was an American anarcho-syndicalist. Most of the book is his translations, which he says are "strict to the meaning, but are not literal" (Preface). The witnesses are mainly Gaston Leval, Augustin Souchy and José Peirats, all of the CNT's own movement, plus H. E. Kaminski, a German-French journalist who visited in 1936.

Money is treated in chapter 5, "The Economics of Revolution" (pp. 65–76), above all Dolgoff's section "Money and Exchange" (pp. 70, 73–76). The village reports in chapters 8–10 add detail. Both "Money and Exchange" and Kaminski's account of Alcora are reproduced in [the extract](../Documents/The_Anarchist_Collectives-money.md).

## Did they abolish money, or make a new one?

Two general statements frame the table. Leval: where the state was strong (Castile, Catalonia, the Levant) the peseta was kept "as the standard of value and unit of distribution"; where it was weak, "above all Aragon, local collectives issued as many as 250 and even 300 different kinds of local money" (p. 74). Peirats: "the first attempts to abolish money and wages had to be abandoned and replaced by the family wage" (p. 74).

Between collectives, and with the outside world, trade was in pesetas or barter. Binefar's district traded 5,000,000 pesetas' worth of goods in three months (p. 148). The Levant peasant federation and the Central Labor Bank of Catalonia (August 1937) settled accounts between collectives "not in cash, but in exchange of goods or services" (pp. 68, 76).

### Dolgoff's answer

Dolgoff argues that "money" is just "a standard for measuring the value of goods and services", like the metric system, so changing its form is not abolishing it (pp. 70, 73). By that test the collectives replaced money rather than abolished it. Mintz, whom he quotes, is blunter: "The agrarian collectives decided to abolish money, only to adopt other systems of exchange" (p. 75).

*My reading:* the local money was mostly a ration coupon, not a currency. It could not be saved up to buy means of production (Kaminski), could not be spent outside the village, and was handed out by family size. Its peseta face value tied it to the old unit of account, though.

### Everyone else was printing money too

Not all the odd money of 1936–37 was anarchist. The Republic withdrew silver coin into a reserve and did not replace the small change. The Generalitat of Catalonia created its own currency by a decree of 21 September 1936 and then let municipalities issue small notes; 773 of its 1,075 municipalities did so. Across the Republican zone some 2,000 local, provincial and regional bodies issued more than 7,000 kinds of note by the end of 1937, about half of them in Catalonia ([vscw.ca](https://vscw.ca/en/node/14)). So a coupon from a Catalan town council and a voucher from an anarchist collective sat side by side. Only the second was meant to replace wages.

## The CNT's producer's card (Zaragoza, May 1936)

The CNT's Fourth Congress met in Zaragoza on 1–10 May 1936: 649 delegates for 982 unions and some 550,000 members. It adopted a long resolution, "Concepto confederal del comunismo libertario". It was based largely on Isaac Puente's pamphlet *El comunismo libertario* (1932). On exchange inside a commune it says:

> En lo que se refiere al aspecto comunal del mismo, bastarán las cartas de productor, extendidas por los Consejos de taller y de fábrica, dando derecho a que aquellos puedan adquirir lo necesario para cubrir sus necesidades. La carta de productor constituye el principio de un signo de cambio, el cual quedará sujeto a estos dos elementos reguladores: Primero, que sea intransferible; segundo, que se adopte un procedimiento mediante el cual en la carta se registre el valor del trabajo por unidades de jornada y este valor tenga el máximo de un año de validez para la adquisición de productos.
>
> A los elementos de la población pasiva serán los Consejos comunales los que les facilitarán las cartas de consumo.

My translation:

> As for the communal side of this, producer's cards will suffice, issued by the workshop and factory councils, entitling the holders to acquire what they need to cover their needs. The producer's card is the beginning of a token of exchange, which will be subject to two regulating conditions: first, that it be non-transferable; second, that a procedure be adopted by which the value of the work is recorded on the card in units of a working day, and this value is valid for at most one year for the acquisition of products.
>
> The communal councils will provide consumer's cards to the non-working population.

The published English versions differ in one phrase. Peirats's translation (in Graham's *Anarchism: A Documentary History*) has "the labour value in working units". The libcom translation has "the value of the day's work that the holder has performed". "Unidades de jornada" means units of a working day, or shift.

The resolution adds at once that this is no "hard and fast norm": a commune may adopt "some other arrangement for internal distribution".

### Compared with Marx and the GIC

| | Marx | GIC, *Fundamental Principles* | CNT, Zaragoza 1936 | Collectives in practice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unit | Hours of labour | The socially average working hour | The working day (*jornada*) | The peseta, or local scrip with a peseta value |
| What sets the amount | Labour given, after deductions for common funds | Hours worked × the payout factor | Labour recorded on the card, but also "what they need to cover their needs" | Size of the family |
| Can it be passed on? | "These vouchers are not money. They do not circulate." | Same (Marx quoted, [ch. 3 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/03_The_Unit_of_Account_in_Communism.md#b-the-socially-average-working-time-by-marx-and-engels)) | Non-transferable | Not valid outside the issuing collective |
| Time limit | None stated | None found | One year at most | None found |
| People not working | Common funds | General social work, paid for through the payout factor ([ch. 10](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor)) | Consumer's cards from the communal council | Included in the family wage |
| Are products priced in the same unit? | Yes: "as much as the same amount of labor cost" | Yes: every product's production time, (P + C) + L | Not addressed | No: in pesetas |

*My reading:*

- **The card is half of the GIC's scheme.** It records what each worker gives in labour time. But nothing in the resolution measures what each product costs in labour time. The GIC's point is that the two go together: "If the individual working time is to be the *measure* for the product to be consumed individually, then the product mass must also be measured with the same measure" ([ch. 3 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/03_The_Unit_of_Account_in_Communism.md#b-the-socially-average-working-time-by-marx-and-engels)). The resolution's "statistical and production councils" collect figures, but they don't price goods.
- **It hedges between two principles.** The card both records labour and entitles the holder to "what they need". Read one way it is Marx's lower phase; read the other it is Kropotkin's "take from the heap" with a work record attached.
- **A working day is a cruder unit than an hour,** and nothing makes it socially average.
- **The one-year limit is new.** Neither Marx nor the GIC has it. Its likely purpose is to stop anyone saving claims. The GIC gets the same result differently: certificates are cancelled when used and don't circulate.
- **Where did the idea come from?** No link to the GIC was found. The Spanish movement had its own source: the anarchist "collectivists" of the 1870s–80s, Bakunin's followers, who held "to each according to their works" until the communists won out after 1882 (Ricardo Mella, *Collectivism and Communism*, 1900). The producer's card looks like a compromise between the two traditions.

### What became of it

The war began ten weeks later. The CNT leadership in Barcelona chose not to proclaim libertarian communism; it worked with the other anti-fascist forces instead. The collectives each made their own arrangements, as the resolution allowed.

The names "producer's card" and "consumer's card" reappear in the February 1937 congress of the Aragon Federation of Collectives (Leval, pp. 167–168). Dolgoff's account of the same congress describes the outcome as a family ration booklet (p. 74). That is a needs-based ration, not a record of labour. Alcora's punched card, with five pesetas of coupons per day worked, is the only case found where daily work was recorded and paid. Even there the coupons were issued per family, and Kaminski notes that the family card put women "under the control of men" (p. 144).

The Aragon collectives lost their regional government when the Republic dissolved the Council of Aragon in August 1937. Enrique Líster's Communist-led division then broke up many of them.

## How the GIC and the council communists saw it

**In the book.** [Ch. 5 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/05_Libertarian_Communism.md#e-anarcho-syndicalism) uses reports of the CNT's June 1931 congress, from the Dutch *De Syndicalist* and the French *La Révolution prolétarienne* (see [the sources file](../fundamental_principles-sources/organisations-and-periodicals.md)). The GIC objects that the CNT, "as a trade union, demands the management and administration of economic life for itself", rather than the workers' councils. It also says the CNT is silent on the economic basis of consumption: "The economic critique of Faure's libertarian communism is also the critique of anarcho-syndicalism."

**On the family wage.** The GIC takes up the very case the family wage was meant to solve: one worker single, another "with a family with five children" ([ch. 9 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#i-fair-distribution)). It concedes that an equal labour-time measure is unfair to the second worker. It keeps the measure anyway, as "the only way for workers to *maintain* control over operational life". Without that measure, "no 'higher instances'" can be avoided: someone has to decide each person's share.

*My reading:* Alcora shows what the GIC feared. Because there was no measure, the committee became the "*pater familias*": "Each special desire should be submitted to it." Money for the cinema came "on holidays, of course. There is no money for vice" (pp. 144–145).

**In 1937.** "Anarchism and the Spanish Revolution" first appeared in the GIC's *Räte-Korrespondenz* and then in Paul Mattick's *International Council Correspondence* (June 1937); libcom attributes it to Helmut Wagner. It applies the *Fundamental Principles* directly:

> In Catalonia this did not happen. Money remains the medium of exchange for products. … The wage system is upheld in Spain. Only one thing has changed: the wage labor that once served the capitalist, now serves the unions.

> … if labor-time is not the measure of production and distribution, but economic activity is regulated by a "statistical bureau" that determines the "ration" that workers are intended, then in that situation a hybrid system of exploitation would be operating.

The article expects that workers would "create a kind of job card … only obtainable by those who perform useful work". The Zaragoza producer's card was close to this, but the article doesn't mention it. It is about Catalonia's industry, not the Aragon villages. The article also criticises the CNT's proposed "credit council" for overlooking "the need for a unit of calculation".

Mattick reviewed Santillán's *After the Revolution* in the same journal (October 1937). He objected that the plan gave statisticians "almost unlimited power over the mass of consumption goods", and that the market would go on regulating supply and demand "much the same as under capitalism".

## Bookchin

Patrick is right that Bookchin adds little here. His introductory essay (dated September 1973, pp. xi–xxxix) is about organisation: the CNT's federal structure, the FAI's affinity groups, and the "peasant" roots of Spanish anarchism. Money comes up once, in praise: the Spanish "even abolished money and lived by communistic principles of work and distribution" (p. xxxix). That is the loose usage Dolgoff's own chapter corrects.

One passage bears on the GIC, though not on money. Bookchin says the free society "must be the free commune, not a confederation of factories, however self-administered; for such a confederation takes a part of society—its economic component—and reifies it into the totality of society" (p. xxxiv). *My reading:* the GIC's society is close to the "confederation of factories" he rejects: operational units, linked by a common accounting. The Zaragoza resolution sides with Bookchin's commune.

*The Spanish Anarchists: The Heroic Years 1868–1936* (1977) ends where the collectives begin. A promised second volume on the war never appeared. Its useful part for this topic is the early history of the collectivist–communist split. Dolgoff reviewed it critically in 1977, and Bookchin replied.

## The war and the factions

On 17–18 July 1936 army officers rose against the Popular Front government elected that February. The rising failed in about half the country, including Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia. Where it failed, the workers' organisations that had defeated it took over factories, land and transport. In Catalonia and Aragon this was led by the CNT. The war ended with Franco's victory on 1 April 1939. Inside the Republican zone the revolution was reversed in stages: the anarchists' entry into government (autumn 1936), the May Days in Barcelona (May 1937), and the dissolution of the Council of Aragon (August 1937).

### The UGT

The Unión General de Trabajadores was the CNT's main rival, and about its size: 1,041,539 members in June 1932. Much of that growth came from its land workers' federation (FNTT, later FETT), which went from 36,639 members in 1930 to 392,953 in 1932. Nothing on labour certificates, or on abolishing money, was found from the UGT, its leaders, or the PSOE. But the UGT did have a view on how people should be paid, and three things are on record.

**Its collectives.** The UGT ran a large share of the land collectives, mostly outside Aragon and Catalonia. Of about 1,300 collectives documented, 27% were the UGT's and 64% the CNT's (Garrido 2010, cited by Garrot). In Castile–La Mancha and Jaén the UGT was the larger force. The FETT wanted collectivisation to be voluntary, and it left family farms alone if they hired no outside labour. On pay, José Luis Garrot's survey says the anarchist collectives used the family wage, often paid in vouchers with an expiry date, while "in the socialist ones the wage usually depended on output (*rendimiento*) and, above all, on the work each collectivist did". That rests on one secondary compilation. The regional studies were not read (see [Open questions](#open-questions)). Joint collectives varied. Alcázar de San Juan (Ciudad Real) was set up by the CNT and UGT in October 1936. It paid a family wage of 8 pesetas a day to the head of the family, plus 50 céntimos for each child under 14. Its alcohol factory paid 10 pesetas a day and its workshops 15 (Garrot, citing Atienza 2006).

**What Dolgoff's witnesses say.** They are hostile to the UGT's leaders but friendly to its members. Leval says that compulsory union membership in Catalonia drove "all the counter- and anti-revolutionary elements" into the UGT. He also says its textile workers, under pressure from their leaders, voted against socialising the industry after earlier backing joint control (pp. 55–56). In Mas de las Matas, 50 UGT families "were instructed by their leaders not to join the collective" (p. 160). The joint CNT–UGT agreement for Asturias (Gijón, January 1937) told the control committees to "improve working conditions and wages" (p. 108). Wages stayed.

**The unity programme of March 1938.** The two unions published a joint *Programa de unidad de acción* on 18 March 1938. It is the one place found where the CNT signed up to a pay system for the whole Republic, and the system is the UGT's, not Zaragoza's. Point 3 of the section on wages, prices and supplies:

> 3° La UGT y la CNT estiman que debe establecerse el salario mínimo, en relación con el coste de la vida, y teniendo en cuenta, de una parte, las categorías profesionales, y de otra, el rendimiento de cada uno. En este sentido defenderán en las industrias el principio de "a más y mejor producción, mayor retribución", sin distinción de sexo, ni de edad, en tanto duran las circunstancias provocadas por necesidades de reconstrucción nacional

My translation: "The UGT and the CNT consider that a minimum wage should be set, related to the cost of living, taking account of skill grades on the one hand and each person's output on the other. To this end they will defend in industry the principle of 'more and better production, more pay', without distinction of sex or age, while the circumstances caused by the needs of national reconstruction last."

The same programme makes three other changes:

- Collectives that do not fit a new law "DEBEN DESAPARECER" (must disappear).
- The land is to be nationalised, and planning goes to a state economic council.
- The family part of the family wage passes to the state: the government is to study "fórmulas de compensación familiar por medio de leyes" (family allowances set by law).

The text is quoted from Manuel Azaretto's transcription in *Las pendientes resbaladizas* (Montevideo, 1939), pp. 29–31.

*My reading:* the UGT's principle, pay for work done, is in one way nearer to Marx than the family wage was: each worker gets back according to what they give. But it measures output and skill grade in pesetas, as a piece-rate wage does. Marx's measure is labour "defined by its duration or intensity", recorded on a certificate that is not money ([Gotha extract](../Documents/Critique_of_the_Gotha_Programme-certificates.md)). By signing the programme, the CNT dropped the Zaragoza producer's card. It also confirmed in writing what the council communists had said in 1937: "The wage system is upheld in Spain."

**The POUM.** Nothing on labour certificates or money turned up in the POUM documents checked on marxists.org: the 1936 programme and "The General Policy of the POUM" (September 1936). The latter calls only for "the transformation of Spain in a socialist direction", through the Catalan Economic Council and the workers' committees.

## Open questions

- **Was the producer's card tried anywhere as written?** The Aragon congress of February 1937 is the place to look; Leval's *Espagne libertaire* and *Né Franco né Stalin* give fuller accounts than Dolgoff prints.
- **Did anyone in the CNT read the GIC?** The *Fundamental Principles* appeared in German (1930) and Dutch (1935). No 1930s Spanish edition was found. A Spanish introduction exists at [left-dis.nl](https://www.left-dis.nl/e/FC_IntrodEspagnol.pdf), but its date was not found.
- **What did Santillán say about money?** Dolgoff quotes only his "Council of Credit and Exchange". *El organismo económico de la revolución* (1936) needs reading in full.
- **The debates before Zaragoza.** Evans and Stainforth (2023) study the competing drafts of libertarian communism before the congress; not read here.
- **How did the UGT's own collectives pay?** The claim that they paid by output rests on Garrot's survey. The regional studies should settle it: Aurora Bosch, *Ugetistas y libertarios* (1983), for Valencia; Luis Garrido González, *Colectividades agrarias en Andalucía: Jaén* (1979); and Natividad Rodrigo González on Castile–La Mancha. The FETT's paper *El Obrero de la Tierra* would give the federation's own line.
- **Should the whole of Dolgoff's book be converted** into `Documents/`? Only the money sections are extracted so far.
