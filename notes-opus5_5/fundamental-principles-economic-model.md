# The economic model of the *Fundamental Principles*

A guide to the GIC's proposal for an economy run on labour time, drawn out of the *[Fundamental Principles of Communist Production and Distribution](../Documents/fundamental_principles/README.md)* without the historical and polemical material. It sorts the chapters, defines the Marxist terms and acronyms, collects every equation with its terms, follows the shoe example through the book, and sets out what the book says about unskilled labour. The last section puts it together as a conceptual model.

This is a research note by Claude (Opus 5.5), written from a brief by Patrick. Points marked *my reading* or *my illustration* are interpretation or construction, not something the book says. Quotations keep the translation's American spelling ("labor").

## Summary

- **The model is in chapters 3, 6–11, 13, 14b and 17 b–f**, plus the closing sections of 12 (e–g). Chapter 16 adds one rule. Chapters 2, 4, 5, 14a, 15 and the appendix are history or polemic. Chapters 7–10 mix the two: each sets out the model, then attacks Kautsky, Leichter or Soviet Hungary.
- The text dates from 1930 (German) and 1935 (Dutch), before the Spanish Revolution of 1936. Spain appears in [5 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/05_Libertarian_Communism.md#e-anarcho-syndicalism), on the CNT's plans at its 1931 congress, and in the 2020 editor's foreword. The bulk of the history is Russia, Soviet Hungary and the German revolution of 1918–23.
- "Historical materialism" appears once, in the [Foreword to the second edition](../Documents/fundamental_principles/00d_Foreword_to_the_Second_Edition.md). "Dialectical materialism", and "dialectic" in any form, never appear.
- **Two acronyms belong to the model:** GSW (general social work) and FIC (factor of individual consumption, also called the payout factor). GIC/GIK is the authors' own name. The others (SEC, OVWR, CPR, NEP) belong to the Russian chapters.
- The production equation is (f + c) + l: **fixed** means of production (machines, buildings), **circulating** means of production (raw materials, consumables), and **labour**. "Variable" never appears as a category. The GIC's split is not Marx's constant and variable capital (see [below](#the-production-equation-is-not-marxs-constant-and-variable-capital)).
- The book uses one shoe factory throughout (ch. 7, 10, 11, 13, 17) and three shoe factories side by side to get the social average (ch. 8). The chain of units, each passing its product on to the next, is stated only in general terms; the book never follows a shoe through it with figures. The section [Following a shoe through the chain](#following-a-shoe-through-the-chain) builds one, from the cattle farm and the ore mine to the consumer cooperative's store, marked as an illustration.
- **On unskilled labour the GIC is blunt:** one hour is one hour, whoever works it. It meets the case for paying skilled work more by making training a social cost, paid out of the GSW budget, rather than a debt the skilled worker recovers through higher pay.

## Marxist terms

### Your two checks

- **"Historical materialism": once.** [Foreword to the second edition](../Documents/fundamental_principles/00d_Foreword_to_the_Second_Edition.md): "This, of course, can never be the intention of the authors, who are based on historical materialism." The point is that the book is not a "plan" to be propagated: ideas come from "the practice of everyday life", and books only put that experience in order. "Materialism" appears nowhere else.
- **"Dialectical materialism": never.** No form of "dialectic" appears. The nearest is [17 f](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#f-centralism---federalism): the contrast between centralism and federalism "was abolished in a higher entity", which is the Hegelian move without the name.

### Old words given new content

The GIC keeps the capitalist word in everyday speech but insists the content has changed, because ownership has. These "transformations of terms" are the core of its argument.

| Capitalist term | GIC term | Why | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exchange | **Transfer** | You must own something to exchange it. Products stay common property as they move. | [7 a](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#a-the-transfer-of-the-goods) |
| Value | **Production time** | Same quantity (average hours), but no longer a property of privately owned commodities. | [7 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#b-the-production-time) |
| Money, wage | **Consumption money** (Marx's "labour certificate", Owen's "labour money") | Valid only for consumer goods, never between units. "No more 'money' than a ticket for the theatre" (Marx). | [9 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#c-the-consumption-money), [11 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/11_Accounting_as_Summary_of_Production_and_Distribution.md#b-giro-transactions-as-settlement) |
| Payment between firms | **Giro settlement** | A bookkeeping entry at the central giro office; nothing circulates. *Giro* is a Continental European word (Dutch and German, from the Italian for "circulation"), and the English translation keeps it untranslated. It means a transfer the payer orders straight from their account into the payee's: in English, a bank transfer or direct credit. The book's own gloss is "a transfer or wiring". In the Netherlands, where the GIC wrote, and in Germany, nearly every adult had a postal giro account, so the authors could take the giro office for granted as the way firms settle "just as in capitalism" ([17 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#e-summary)). The English-speaking world paid by cheque instead; Britain got a Post Office giro only in 1968, and in the US, Canada and Australia the word never came into everyday use. So the term is Eurocentric: a Continental payment system is presented as simply how settlement works. Read "giro office" as "the central clearing house where every unit holds its account". | [11 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/11_Accounting_as_Summary_of_Production_and_Distribution.md#b-giro-transactions-as-settlement) |
| Income / expenditure (credit / debit) | **Transferred to / withdrawn from the community** | The unit owns neither inputs nor output; it only records flows. | [11 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/11_Accounting_as_Summary_of_Production_and_Distribution.md#c-transformation-of-terms-no-income---no-expenditure) |
| Profit / loss | **Rationality** (production time above or below the social average) | A unit returns exactly what it takes, in another form. "Profitability is unknown." | [11 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/11_Accounting_as_Summary_of_Production_and_Distribution.md#d-transformation-of-terms-no-gain-no-loss78) |
| Market | **Cooperatives linked directly to production** | The market is abolished as a *legal* relation (private ownership). The movement of goods continues. | [12 e–g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/12_The_Abolition_of_the_Market.md#e-the-abolition-of-the-market-in-the-marxist-sense) |
| Tax | **Payout factor** (deduction before certificates are issued) | Social costs come off hours, never added to prices. | [10 f–g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#f-the-marxist-solution) |

### "Operational unit" and its family

"Operational unit" is the translation's word for the workplace: a factory, mine, railway, hospital, council office. *My reading*: it renders the German *Betrieb* (Dutch *bedrijf*), meaning an enterprise or establishment, a word without the "firm owned by someone" sense of English "company". The English edition sometimes slips into "company", "plant" and "operation" for the same thing. Related terms:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Operational unit** | The basic cell. Keeps its own books on (f + c) + l and passes its product on. 156 uses. |
| **Productive operational unit** | Hands its product to consumers in exchange for consumption money ([10 a](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#a-two-forms-of-distribution)). Includes transport and other services that are not "free". |
| **Public operational unit / GSW unit** | Hands its product or service out free, "taking as needed": health, education, councils, parks, the accounting office itself. |
| **Mixed operational unit** | Free for households, charged to other units. Example: a power station ([10 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#i-mixed-operational-units)). |
| **Operational organisation** (also "factory organisation") | The workers of a unit organised to run it: the body that holds the right of disposal. Federated, they form the workers' councils. |
| **Industrial branch / sector** | All units making the same product, linked by accounts ("horizontal consolidation") to find the social average ([8 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#d-the-difference-in-company-productivity)). |
| **Operational life** | The economy as a whole (German *Betriebsleben*, *my reading*). |

## The equations

The book's letters, all measured in **hours of socially average labour**:

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| f | "Fixed" means of production used up in one unit: wear and tear on machines and buildings |
| c | "Circulating" means of production used up in one unit: raw materials and consumables |
| l | Living labour: hours worked in the unit |
| F, C, L | The same, summed over all **productive** units |
| index <sub>t</sub> | Total, over all units, productive and public |
| index <sub>p</sub> | Public (GSW) units |
| F'<sub>p</sub>, C'<sub>p</sub> | The part of a mixed public unit's output that goes to other units as means of production or raw material |
| P | In 10 g only, a misprint or mistranslation for C (see E6) |

### E1. The production equation (one unit)

> f + c + l = product
> (machinery and buildings) + (raw materials, consumables) + (labor) = product

[7 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#d-communist-reproduction). Every unit, "whether it is a sugar factory, a railway company or an administrative body", keeps its books this way. The shoe factory:

> 1,250 working hours + 61,250 working hours + 62,500 working hours = 125,000 working hours
> machines etc. + raw materials + work = 40,000 pairs of shoes
> That's an average of 3.125 hours per pair.

It is also a **reproduction formula**: to run again next period, the factory must take back 1,250 hours of equipment, 61,250 of raw materials, and its workers must be able to consume 62,500 hours of goods.

### E2. The whole economy

> (F<sub>t</sub> + C<sub>t</sub>) + L<sub>t</sub> = Total product
> 108 million + 650 million + 650 million = 1,408 million working hours

[7 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#d-communist-reproduction). The sum of E1 over every unit. These figures are used again in chapters 10 and 13 and are consistent: productive units 100 + 600 + 600 = 1,300 million, public units 8 + 50 + 50 = 108 million.

### E3. The social average production time

> The entire industry produces 205,000 pairs of shoes in 652,500 hours.
> That is per pair: 652,500 / 205,000 equals 3.18 hours.

[8 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#d-the-difference-in-company-productivity). In general:

social average = Σ (f + c + l) over all units in the branch ÷ Σ units of product

| | Pairs | Hours per pair (operational average) | Hours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plant 1 | 40,000 | 3.125 | 125,000 |
| Plant 2 | 65,000 | 3.5 | 227,500 |
| Plant 3 | 100,000 | 3 | 300,000 |
| **Branch** | **205,000** | **3.18** (social average) | **652,500** |

Every pair passes on at 3.18 hours, whichever plant made it. Plants 1 and 3 show hours "over" and plant 2 a matching "deficit"; across the branch they cancel, so the branch's books "must *always* be 'balanced'". The deviation is not a profit or loss but a signal: the branch may ask plant 2 *why* it takes longer ([11 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/11_Accounting_as_Summary_of_Production_and_Distribution.md#d-transformation-of-terms-no-gain-no-loss78)).

### E4. Leichter's surcharge (rejected)

> f + c + l + GSW = price
> 1,250 + 61,250 + (62,500 × 1.166) = 135,375
> This is an average of 3.384 hours per pair.

[10 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#d-leichter-solution). Otto Leichter's way of funding public services: GSW budget ÷ all hours worked = 108 / 650 = 0.166, added to the labour of every product. The GIC rejects it because the price no longer shows "how much work each product requires", and because a surcharge invites "price policy". It also says the calculation would raise more than the social costs, without showing the arithmetic.

### E5. The GSW budget

> (f<sub>p</sub> + c<sub>p</sub>) + l<sub>p</sub> (one public unit)
> F<sub>p</sub> + C<sub>p</sub> + L<sub>p</sub> = GSW budget
> 8 million + 50 million + 50 million = 108 million working hours

[10 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#b-the-gsw-budget). The same equation as E1, with no product to hand on. Set in advance: "the amount of work that the society wants to make available to the public operational units for the current year".

### E6. The productive units' budget

> (F + P) + L = product mass
> 100 million + 600 million + 600 million = 1,300 million working hours

[10 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor). **P here means C** (raw materials). After the productive units take back their F and C, 600 million hours of product are left: equal to L, the hours they worked.

### E7. The payout factor (FIC)

> FIC = (L − (F<sub>p</sub> + C<sub>p</sub>)) / (L + L<sub>p</sub>)
> FIC = (600 − 58) / (600 + 50) = 542 / 650 = 0.83

[10 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor). (The repository file writes this in LaTeX; the book's terms are the same.)

- **Top line**: the product left for individual consumption. The 600 million hours left over from E6, less the 58 million of means of production and raw materials the public units take.
- **Bottom line**: every hour worked, in productive *and* public units. Teachers and nurses receive certificates like everyone else.
- **Result**: 40 hours of work give 0.83 × 40 = 33.2 hours of consumption money. Total claims: 0.83 × 650 = 542, exactly the product available.

"Nobody 'assigns' anything to anyone": the factor results from "a simple summation in the Giro Office". Everyone has the same factor; a district that votes itself extra public services (reading rooms, say) has a lower one, "a 'local tax'" ([10 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#h-the-growth-process-of-communism)).

### E8. The payout factor with mixed units

> FIC = (L − {(F<sub>p</sub> + C<sub>p</sub>) − (F'<sub>p</sub> + C'<sub>p</sub>)}) / (L + L<sub>p</sub>)

[10 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#i-mixed-operational-units). A mixed unit's supplies to other units are charged to them, so only the part of its inputs used for free household supply comes off the consumption fund. Freight transport "should never be included in the 'take as needed' category, as a final product is a consumable only at its destination."

### E9. Expansion of production

> 1.1 x (F<sub>t</sub> + C<sub>t</sub>) + L<sub>t</sub> = total production

[13 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#c-the-general-rule-for-the-expansion-of-the-operational-unit). The congress of workers' councils sets a ceiling (the example is 10 %) on how far any unit may enlarge its means of production and raw materials. The shoe factory's f + c is 62,500 hours, so it may take 6,250 more: next year's books show 62,500 + 6,250 "taken from society".

### E10. The payout factor with expansion

> 10 % of 758 million working hours equal to 75.8 million working hours. This amount must be paid by all employees together so that 75.8 / 650 = 0.12 of their consumption. … This is now 0.83 − 0.12 = 0.71 *with* operational expansion. For a working week of 40 hours, everyone receives 0.71 x 40 = 28.4 hours of consumption.

[13 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#e-the-influence-on-the-payout-factor). *My reading*, as one formula, with *a* the expansion rate:

FIC = (L − (F<sub>p</sub> + C<sub>p</sub>) − a(F<sub>t</sub> + C<sub>t</sub>)) / (L + L<sub>p</sub>) = (600 − 58 − 75.8) / 650 = 0.717

The book's 0.71 comes from rounding before subtracting.

Two further deductions come off the same factor, without figures: **special accumulation**, multi-year works like railways and sea walls, budgeted as GSW ([13 f](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#f-special-accumulation)); and a **general fund** stockpiled against floods and other disasters ([13 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#g-the-general-fund)).

### E11. The key number (converting money to hours)

> If this average turns out to be 0.80 Dollar 1 working hour, then each operational unit can calculate a provisional production time for its product. It is now possible in all operational units to convert the total stock into working hours by multiplying all monetary amounts expressed in marks by 1¼. This figure is the key figure.
>
> f = used tools, buildings, etc. Mark 1,000 = 1,250 working hours
> c = leather and so on Mark 49,000 = 61,250 working hours
> l = hours worked 62,500

[17 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#c-the-key-number). Hours = money × key number, where key number = 1 ÷ (money per hour) = 1 ÷ 0.80 = 1.25. It is found from mass-produced goods (coal, electricity, iron, potash) whose tonnage and hours are known, leaving out interest. This is a one-off conversion at the start, like the 1923 switch from paper marks to gold marks; once units keep their books in hours, the real production times take over. The passage mixes guilders, dollars and marks; the arithmetic is the same whichever is meant.

### The production equation is not Marx's constant and variable capital

*My reading.* Marx splits a commodity's value into constant capital (c, means of production), variable capital (v, wages) and surplus value (s). The GIC's split is different:

| GIC | Marx |
| --- | --- |
| f (fixed) + c (circulating) | c (constant capital), split into fixed and circulating |
| l (living labour) | v + s (wages plus surplus value) |

There is no v or s because there is no wage and no surplus value: all of l is the hours worked, and all of it comes back to workers, less the deductions made through the FIC.

### What the book says about the chain

The idea that a product moves from unit to unit, each adding its step, is there, but in general terms:

> In communism, operational units are equal parts of a closed whole, of the entire production and distribution process. Each operational unit carries out only one partial activity by passing on its product to the other until it is suitable for consumption.

([7 a](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#a-the-transfer-of-the-goods))

> Each "independent" unit has an "end product," and by applying the formula (f+c)+l, it can calculate at any time how much work is necessary for its product. Finally, when the "final plant" has finished its "final product" so that it can be consumed, we know immediately how much work it has involved "from the beginning to the final product, including transport and other ancillary activities". Just as production is made up of sub-processes, the calculation of working time is also made up …

([8 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#c-the-progress))

> if it is to be *transferred* to another company as f or c (as a means of production or as a raw material), the "costs" for this other operational unit are reduced so that it can also work "cheaper". In this way, the shortening of the social production time in a sector spreads to the entire economy without disturbing the calculations of others.

([8 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#e-the-progress-of-technology))

So one unit's whole product (f + c + l) becomes part of the next unit's c (or f, if it is a machine). No unit has to know the history of its inputs; it reads their production time off the giro record. That is the GIC's answer to Kautsky, who thought only a central statistical office could total the labour in a finished pair of shoes.

The book never runs the shoe through the chain with figures. The 61,250 hours of "raw materials" are given as a single number.

### Following a shoe through the chain

*My illustration*, built to fit the book's figures. Only the shoe factory's totals (f 1,250, c 61,250, l 62,500, 40,000 pairs, branch average 3.18) are the book's. Every other unit and number is invented. The figures for each supplier are the share of its output that goes into this batch of 40,000 pairs, not its whole year.

#### Upstream: what goes into the factory

Even a plain leather shoe draws on many branches. Each arrow below is a transfer recorded at the giro. Each "→" between two places is also a freight leg: a transport unit with its own (f + c) + l.

```
UPPER LEATHER    cattle farm → slaughterhouse → tannery ─────────────────────┐
                 (tanning agents, dyes: mine/refinery → chemical works ──► tannery)
LINING           cotton farm → gin → spinning mill → weaving mill ───────────┤
SOLES            oil field → refinery → synthetic rubber plant               │
                   → sole moulding works ────────────────────────────────────┤
THREAD           cotton or polyester → spinning mill → thread works ─────────┤
ADHESIVE         refinery → chemical works ──────────────────────────────────┤
EYELETS, RIVETS  ore mine → smelter → rolling mill → stamping works ─────────┤
LACES            spinning mill → braiding works ─────────────────────────────┤
SHOE BOXES       forest → pulp mill → cardboard works → box works ───────────┤
ELECTRICITY      coal mine or dam → power station (a mixed unit: charged     │
                   to factories, free to households, 10 i) ──────────────────┤

```

Machines (sewing, lasting and cutting machines) come from machine-building works with chains of their own. They enter the shoe as f, only as the wear on them in this period: 1,250 hours.

| Soles | 14,000 | sole moulding works |

| Shoe boxes | 2,500 | box works |

| | Hours |

| f: wear on drums, vats, building | 1,000 |
| c: hides from the slaughterhouse | 14,000 |

The slaughterhouse in turn charges hides at a production time that already contains the cattle farm's feed, buildings and work, and the freight between them. No unit needs to know any of that history. Each one reads the production time of its inputs off the giro record and adds its own f, c and l.

#### Downstream: from the factory to the worker

| Step | Hours per pair | Running total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shoe factory, at the branch social average (the factory's own figure is 3.125) | 3.18 | 3.18 |
| Freight to a regional distribution centre | 0.05 | 3.23 |
| Distribution centre (a warehouse: storage, sorting, handling) | 0.10 | 3.33 |
| Freight to the store | 0.05 | 3.38 |
| Consumer cooperative's store: its share of the store's f + c + l | 0.20 | **3.58** |

The worker hands over 3.58 hours of consumption money for the pair. At FIC = 0.83, a 40-hour week gives 33.2 hours of certificates, enough for about nine pairs.

#### What "cooperative" means here

In the book, "cooperative" means a **consumer cooperative**. It does two jobs:

- **It is the shop.** "Consumers receive their products in their cooperative for consumption money and have complete freedom in the choice of goods" ([12 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/12_The_Abolition_of_the_Market.md#e-the-abolition-of-the-market-in-the-marxist-sense)). So it is a general store or shopping centre stocking shoes, bread, books and the rest, not a shoe shop.
- **It is the consumers' organisation.** "In these cooperatives, the individual wishes of the consumers are expressed jointly … the 'middlemen' disappear and the cooperatives are directly linked to the operational units" ([12 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/12_The_Abolition_of_the_Market.md#g-the-cooperatives-and-the-benchmark-of-needs)). Its orders are how needs reach production.

The book says distribution is done by "all kinds of cooperatives" but goes no further. Warehouses, distribution centres and wholesale are not mentioned; how the organisations connect "cannot be examined in advance" ([17 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#d-utopism)). *My reading*: a distribution centre would be an operational unit of its own, keeping the same books, and the store would be a second one run by (or for) the consumers' cooperative.

#### What the fuller chain brings out

- **Each box keeps the same equation.** That is the whole of the GIC's answer to Kautsky, who thought only a central statistical office could total the labour in a finished pair of shoes ([8 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#c-the-progress)).
- **What passes on is the branch's social average, not the unit's own figure.** The shoes go on at 3.18 hours a pair, not this factory's 3.125. The same holds for leather, soles and rivets, each at its own branch's average.
- **Transport is everywhere, and never free.** Freight sits between almost every pair of units and is part of what the consumer pays. The book is firm that "the transport of goods should never be included in the 'take as needed' category, as a final product is a consumable only at its destination" ([10 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#i-mixed-operational-units)).
- **The chain is a network, not a ladder.** The tannery also supplies upholsterers and bag makers. The chemical works supplies the tannery *and* the shoe factory. The power station supplies everyone. "Lower" and "higher" units are a fair picture for one product only.
- **Two accounting problems the book does not discuss** (*my reading*):
  - **Joint products.** A slaughterhouse produces meat and hides in one process. How its hours are split between them decides how many hours the leather carries. See the next section.
  - **Shared overheads.** A store or distribution centre handles thousands of products. Its hours must be spread across them by some rule (per item, per volume, per shelf-day). The GIC praises capitalist cost accounting for already handling "expenses and expenditures that are not directly included in the exchangeable end product" ([8 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#b-leichters-answer)), and seems to assume the same methods carry over.

#### Joint products: meat, milk and hides

**Where hides come from.** Cattle are not bred for leather. Hides come from beef cattle at slaughter and from dairy cows culled from the herd, and calfskin from the male calves of dairy herds. The exceptions are exotic leathers: crocodiles, alligators and ostriches are farmed largely for their skins. Nothing much of the animal is wasted: what people don't eat goes to pet food and rendering (tallow, bone meal, gelatin).

The hide is a small and shrinking part of the animal's value. In the US hides have historically been about 6–8 % of the value of beef cattle, and about half the value of the by-products. By 2020 they were reported at little more than 1 % of the animal's value. The Leather and Hide Council of America (an industry body) estimated that about 5.5 million US hides went to landfill in 2019, and about 16 % of all US hides in 2020, because no one would buy them. A study by Brester and Swanser found that hide prices have no direct influence on cattle numbers: nobody raises more cattle because leather is dear. (Industry sources; treat the exact figures with caution.)

**Why this is a problem for the GIC.** One process, raising and killing an animal, yields meat, milk, calves, hides, tallow and bone at once. The book's rule is that every product carries its production time, but it says nothing about how a unit that turns out several products at once divides its hours between them. Production time is supposed to be a fact read off the books. For joint products it is partly a decision.

**Splitting the unit into sub-units.** The GIC's own method points this way. "The producers determine the working time for each sub-process" ([8 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#e-the-progress-of-technology)), and the book praises capitalist cost accounting for finding the cost "of each individual operation … in which of several workshops … on which of several machines" ([8 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#b-leichters-answer)). A slaughterhouse and the farms behind it could keep books by stage:

| Stage | What it produces | How its hours can be charged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raising the animal (farm) | meat, hide, and on dairy farms milk and calves | joint |
| Stunning, killing, bleeding | everything | joint |
| Dehiding | needed for both: the carcass can't be cut until the hide is off | joint |
| Butchering, chilling, packing | meat | meat only |
| Fleshing, salting, storing, loading hides | hide | hide only |
| Rendering | tallow, bone meal | their own |

Sub-units work up to what accountants call the **split-off point**, the moment hide and carcass are separate. After it, every hour can be charged directly. Before it, no amount of subdivision helps, because those hours produced both products at once. A cow's two years of feed and care cannot be divided between its meat and its skin by asking who did the work. And the joint part is mostly upstream: the farm's feed, buildings and labour are far larger than the few minutes of killing. Sub-units shrink the problem but leave its largest part.

**The conventions available.** Capitalist cost accounting has three standard ways to divide joint costs:

| Method | How it divides joint costs | Usable by the GIC? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales value or net realisable value | In proportion to what each product sells for, less its costs after split-off | **No.** There are no prices independent of hours; using production time to set production time is circular. |
| Physical measure | By weight, volume or count at split-off | **Yes.** But by weight a hide would carry a large share of the animal's hours. |
| By-product method | The minor product carries only its own costs after split-off (or its sale value is deducted from the main product's cost); all joint costs go to the main product | **Yes,** in an hours-only form: the hide carries only its post-split-off hours, and all joint hours go to meat (and milk). |

International accounting practice treats most by-products this way: "Most by-products, by their nature, are immaterial. When this is the case, they are often measured at net realisable value and this value is deducted from the cost of the main product" (IAS 2, *Inventories*, para. 14).

**What the choice changes** (*my calculation*). The shoe chain above charges the tannery 14,000 hours for hides, which assumes the hides carry a share of the joint hours. Under the by-product convention they might carry only the slaughterhouse's post-split-off hours, say 2,000. The tannery's leather would then go on at 18,000 hours instead of 30,000, and the 40,000 pairs would carry 12,000 hours less: about 0.3 hours a pair, 3.28 at the counter instead of 3.58. A worker's week would buy ten pairs instead of nine. Nothing about the shoe has changed except an accounting convention.

**Which convention fits** (*my reading*). The by-product convention matches the facts while hides are surplus. If hides are being landfilled, an extra hide costs society almost nothing beyond salting and handling, and leather should be cheap. It stops fitting if leather demand grows beyond the hides that meat and dairy supply. Extra cattle would then be raised partly for their hides, and leather would really be costing feed and care. The by-product convention would hide that cost. The question the books need to answer is what an *extra* hide costs, and that depends on whether hides are in surplus. So the convention would need setting and reviewing, by the industry's units or by the congress of workers' councils, much as the congress sets the 10 % expansion ceiling ([13 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#c-the-general-rule-for-the-expansion-of-the-operational-unit)). Each unit's books would also need to state which convention its figures rest on, so that the public record shows where production time is measured and where it is agreed.

**The theory: Steedman's *Marx after Sraffa*.** Ian Steedman's *Marx after Sraffa* (1977) is a critique of the labour theory of value. Its argument on joint production is the one that bears on this question. Steedman works out labour values directly from physical inputs and hours worked. Where one process yields two products, a product's labour can only be found by treating several processes as a set of simultaneous equations, and the solution can give negative labour values. Morishima and Wolfstetter replied that Steedman had applied Marx's definition to a case Marx did not consider. An unpublished 1978 paper by Hengstenberg and Fay, "Joint production and 'negative values': The weakness of Steedman's attack on the labour theory of value", argues the same side.

*My reading*: the GIC does not calculate labour values as a system of equations. Each unit adds up the hours it actually used, so it cannot produce Steedman's negative values. The price is that, for joint products, the split between products is a convention rather than a measurement. Steedman's argument shows why no measurement can settle it.

**Worth investigating:**

- How Soviet and Comecon planners costed joint products (meat and hides, oil refining, coke and gas) in their material balances.
- Whether Cockshott and Cottrell, who compute labour values from input-output tables, discuss joint production, and which convention they use.
- Morishima's alternative definition of labour values (the minimum labour needed, found by linear programming) and whether it suits a GIC-style system.
- Other joint products, starting with one the book names: the sugar factory ([7 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#d-communist-reproduction)), which yields sugar, molasses and beet pulp. Oil refining is the large modern case.

## Unskilled and skilled labour

### What the GIC says

The book's position is equal hours for all labour. Its commentary on unskilled labour comes in three places.

**1. The opponents' scheme ([7 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#e-reproduction-of-labor-power)).** The GIC sets out the case it rejects, in figures:

> For example, unskilled, learned and intellectual work may be *evaluated* differently. The distribution could be, for example, that the unskilled person is paid 3/4 hours for an hour worked, the learned person just one hour, the civil servant 1 1/2 hours, and the manager 3 hours. A 40-hour week is recorded in the company books: 30 hours for unskilled workers, 40 hours for skilled workers, 60 hours for civil servants, and 120 hours for managers.

It attributes this to Neurath ("life circumstances"), Kautsky (heavy or unpleasant work should earn more, and pay should rise with routine within a trade, including piece rates) and Leichter (a "nutrition physiologist" sets the unskilled worker's subsistence minimum and collective agreements set higher rates for the skilled). Its objection is political, not technical: Leichter is right that wage scales would not stop labour-time accounting, but any scale means "the struggle for the distribution of the product is continued. This struggle is and will be a struggle for power."

**2. Why skill differentials exist ([7 f–g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#f-the-value-of-labor-power-in-life-circumstances-communism)).** Under capitalism, labour power is a commodity, priced at what it costs to reproduce. For the unskilled worker that is the subsistence minimum; their children "cannot learn a profession because they have to earn as much as possible immediately. Thus, the unskilled have themselves reproduced the unskilled labor power again." Skilled labour costs more to reproduce, because of training. Leichter carries this logic into socialism: "Differently qualified workers … need a different amount of effort to reproduce their labor power … All this must be included in the various assessments of the labor power." The GIC quotes Marx's *Wage Labour and Capital* to show this is exactly the capitalist wage law.

The GIC's answer: in communism the reproduction of labour power, including training, becomes "a social function". "Teaching is no longer tied to Papa's wallet but depends solely on the child's disposition and physical condition." Training is paid for collectively (through the GSW budget, *my reading*), so there is no private cost for higher pay to repay. And natural talent earns no extra share: those "endowed by nature with more favorable hereditary factors" already enjoy more of culture and science, without "on top of that even a larger share of the social product".

**3. Workers' own prejudice ([9 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#h-the-pricing-policy-in-hungary), [9 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#i-fair-distribution)).** In Soviet Hungary the workers themselves rejected equal distribution:

> We are familiar with this ideology, which makes the skilled look contemptuously at the unskilled, while at the same time, it runs counter to their legal sense that the holders of the intellectual professions, such as doctors and engineers, should not receive a larger share of the social product. There is a certain conviction that the difference is too great today, but ... a doctor is not a garbage collector. … So much is certain that this change must take place quickly after the revolution because an unequal distribution of the product always leads to disputes within the working class itself.

And in 9 i the conclusion: "all functions in social life, whether mental or manual, should be given equal rights to the wealth of society." The GIC admits this is "unjust" (a single worker and a parent of five get the same) but says equal hours is a *political* demand, "the only way for workers to *maintain* control over operational life".

### What it does not discuss (*my reading*)

- **Marx's "complex labour".** In *Capital* vol. 1, ch. 1, Marx counts skilled labour as "intensified, or rather, multiplied simple labour", so an hour of skilled work may count as several hours of simple work *in the value of the product*. The GIC never mentions this. Its model counts every hour as one both in the product's production time and in the worker's certificates. The two questions are separable (a product could carry multiplied hours while the worker still gets one hour per hour), but the book does not separate them.
- **Intensity within a unit.** The unit's production time is compared with the branch average, so slow *units* show up. A slow or fast *worker* within a unit does not: all hours count the same. The book does not discuss this.
- **Heavy or unpleasant work.** Kautsky's point is answered politically, not practically. The book does not say how unpopular jobs would be filled.

## The conceptual model

### Actors

| Actor | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Worker** | Works hours in an operational unit; receives consumption money = hours × FIC; spends it on consumer goods at production time; uses public services free. |
| **Operational organisation** (works council) | Runs one unit. Keeps its (f + c) + l books; draws inputs and passes on output via the giro; may expand within the congress's limit. |
| **Productive unit** | Passes its product on at production time: to other units through the giro, or to consumers for consumption money. |
| **Public (GSW) unit** | Provides free services within a budget set in advance. |
| **Mixed unit** | Free to households, charged to other units. |
| **Industrial branch** | Pools the accounts of like units to find the social average; asks units well above it to explain. |
| **Consumer cooperative** | Both the consumers' organisation and their store: a general store for all consumer goods, where consumption money is spent. Passes consumers' wishes directly to the producing units. |
| **Giro office (general social accounting)** | Records every transfer between units. Computes the social averages and the FIC as by-products. Has "*not* the leadership, *not* the administration, *not* the power of control". |
| **Congress of workers' councils** | Sets the general rules: the expansion ceiling, the GSW budget, large projects, the general fund, the production plan's framework. |

### Rules

The GIC calls these the "generally valid rules" that the revolution must enforce ([13 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#c-the-general-rule-for-the-expansion-of-the-operational-unit), [14b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/14b_The_Control_of_Operational_Life_II.md#b-control-under-communism), [16](../Documents/fundamental_principles/16_The_Economic_Dictatorship_of_the_Proletariat.md)):

1. **Every unit calculates the production time of its product**, (f + c) + l, in socially average hours.
2. **Working time is the measure of individual consumption**: one hour worked = one hour of claim, times the FIC, for everyone.
3. **Expansion follows a fixed rule** set by the congress (e.g. no more than 10 % a year).
4. **Units stay within the production plan and their budgets.**
5. **All transfers go through the giro.** Consumption money buys only consumer goods. A unit outside the giro gets no raw materials.

### Flows in one period

```
                     ┌───────────────── social stock of goods ──────────────────┐
                     │            (F_t + C_t) + L_t = 1,408 million hours        │
                     └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                          │                    │                       │
       replace own f + c  │   GSW budget f + c │     individual        │
       (+ expansion)      │                    │     consumption       │
                          ▼                    ▼                       ▼
             PRODUCTIVE UNITS          PUBLIC (GSW) UNITS          WORKERS
             100 + 600 = 700           8 + 50 = 58                 542 million
                          │                    │                       ▲
             600 million hours         50 million hours               │
             worked  ─────────────────┬──────────────────              │
                                      ▼                                │
                     certificates = hours × FIC ──────────────────────┘
                     FIC = (600 − 58) / (600 + 50) = 0.83
```

In words:

1. Each unit takes means of production and raw materials from the social stock and records them as "withdrawn from the community".
2. Its workers work; the hours are recorded.
3. It hands on its product at the branch's social average production time, recorded as "transferred to the community".
4. The giro office totals everything. From the totals it knows the FIC.
5. Workers receive consumption money: hours worked × FIC. They spend it at cooperatives on goods priced at production time. Public services are free.
6. Expansion, large projects and the disaster fund lower the FIC; they are never added to prices.
7. Technical progress lowers a product's production time, which lowers the c of every unit downstream, which lowers prices for consumers. With certificates fixed to hours, the gain "directly falls **automatically** to all consumers, without anyone **assigning** anything" ([9 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#e-the-wage-by-leichter)).
8. Over time more branches become free ("taking as needed"). The FIC falls, and labour time measures less and less of consumption.

### Control

"Production is controlled by reproduction" ([14b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/14b_The_Control_of_Operational_Life_II.md#b-control-under-communism)). A unit that takes more hours than the social average for its output cannot replace its inputs, and the books show it at once. If the social average is set too high, more hours are transferred than consumed; too low, and units cannot reproduce. Either shows up as an imbalance. Public units have no such check; they can only be held to their budget, and compared by measures like hours per kilometre of tram or per pupil.

### Starting the system

On the first day there are only money accounts. A **key number** converts them to hours ([17 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#c-the-key-number), E11). Real production times replace the estimates as the books fill up.

### What the book leaves open

The GIC says outright that some things fall outside its "fundamental principles". *My reading* of the gaps that matter for a model:

- **Planning.** The production plan "grows" out of cooperative demand and units' proposals ([14b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/14b_The_Control_of_Operational_Life_II.md#b-control-under-communism)), but how it is drawn up "does not fall within the scope of this writing" ([7 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#b-the-production-time)).
- **Shortage.** Prices are fixed at production time and do not move with demand. When demand exceeds supply, the book says production expands within the plan; it does not say who gets the shoes meanwhile.
- **Timing of the FIC.** The example uses a year's totals. The book notes that distribution happens "at every minute of the day", but not how a running FIC is set before the year's totals are known.
- **Foreign trade.** Mentioned once, as the "'international' movement of goods" that price surcharges would confuse ([10 f](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#f-the-marxist-solution)).
- **Unpaid work, natural resources, environment.** None appears in f, c or l. See [General social work and productive work](general-social-work.md) and [Carbon and labour time](carbon-and-labour-time.md).
