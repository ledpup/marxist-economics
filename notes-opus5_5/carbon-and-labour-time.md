# Carbon and labour time

Patrick's proposal: record fuel and emissions alongside labour time through the whole chain of production, as public bookkeeping for community decisions. This note sets out the proposal, checks its factual claims, surveys what Marxists and other planners have written on the question, and sketches how the GIC's books could carry the record.

This is a research note by Claude (Opus 5.5), written from a brief by Patrick. Points marked *my reading* or *my calculation* are interpretation or arithmetic, not something the sources say.

## Patrick's brief

> Research work done by communists/Marxists around tracking carbon, especially along the lines of "Fundamental Principles...". My idea: fuel-type and quantity needs to be recorded, as separate but equal quality to labour time, throughout the whole process of production. Carbon tracking is for book-keeping purposes for community decision making. We could have a simple ratio: labour time to carbon-used. The ratio is important because an activity high in labour time but low in carbon may be considered worth keep when compared with a similar activity low in labour time but high in carbon. For example: a bicycle courier, a small electric scooter or an ICE car. Each of those could be used to move small packages around. Each of those may have utility in some circumstances, but we **really** want to know how much labour time is used **and** how much carbon. It is to be public record. Note: this has nothing to do with trade. This is book-keeping and analysis for community decision making.
>
> Consider CO2 equivalent. I don't believe it is sufficient. Methane is different to CO2 in the atmosphere. We need to consider aspects like fugitive emissions. Coal does not have fugitive emissions. However, methane, when burnt, yields more energy per molecule than straight carbon (a simplified notion of coal). I believe CO2e can be calculated at any point along the chain of production, if we retain the fuel-type and quantity.
>
> Justification: capitalists have treated the atmosphere as an open sewer for hundreds of years. Atmospheric CO2 continues to accumulate at an accelerating rate. Capitalists have no plan worth considering - their IPCC has had 30+ years to try something and we've seen no change in the acceleration. We are facing ever escalating and accelerating heat, drought, floods, ice-loss, sea-level rise (the least concerning), amongst other predicted outcomes.

## Summary

- **No one has proposed exactly this.** Nothing found adds a fuel or emissions record to the GIC's scheme. The nearest work splits two ways. Cockshott, Cottrell and Dapprich (2022) fold emissions into a single combined value, which their critics call carbon pricing by another name. Neurath's heirs (Vettese and Pendergrass, Neel and Chavez, Benanav) keep many physical measures and refuse any single unit. Patrick's proposal sits between them: labour time stays the only unit of account, and the carbon record is kept beside it, never converted into it.
- **Cockshott and Cottrell's own 1993 position is close to Patrick's.** *Towards a New Socialism* says ecological destruction follows from "any decision mechanism based upon a single objective function", and that environmental questions are "political, not economic".
- **Three of the factual claims hold; one doesn't.** CO<sub>2</sub>e alone is not enough (the IPCC itself says the choice of metric depends on the purpose). Methane yields more energy per carbon atom than coal. CO<sub>2</sub>e can be worked out later from retained quantities. But **coal does have fugitive emissions**: in 2025 coal mining released 43 Mt of methane, more than the natural gas industry's 36 Mt (IEA).
- **Fuel type and quantity are not quite enough.** They fix combustion CO<sub>2</sub>. They don't fix fugitive methane, which depends on the particular mine or gas field, or the roughly quarter of global emissions that come from no fuel at all (cement, fertiliser, livestock, land clearing). The record needs to hold quantities of each **gas released**, entered where it is released, as well as each fuel burned.
- **The GIC's books already have the right shape.** Every operational unit records what flows in and out and passes the totals on by "transfer". A fuel and gas record can travel down the chain exactly as production time does.
- **The useful ratio compares options.** One activity's own ratio of hours to carbon says little. The ratio of the *differences* between two options does: how many kilograms of CO<sub>2</sub>e a choice spends to save one hour of work.

## Checking the claims

### "CO2e is not sufficient"

Correct, and the IPCC agrees in substance. A CO<sub>2</sub>-equivalent converts another gas into the mass of CO<sub>2</sub> that would cause the same warming, but "the same" depends on the period chosen, because the gases behave differently:

| Gas | Time in atmosphere | GWP over 100 years | GWP over 20 years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CO<sub>2</sub> | centuries to millennia (much of it is never removed on human timescales) | 1 | 1 |
| CH<sub>4</sub> from fossil sources | about 12 years | 29.8 | 82.5 |
| CH<sub>4</sub> from other sources | about 12 years | 27.0 | 79.7 |
| N<sub>2</sub>O | about 109 years | 273 | 273 |

(IPCC AR6 WG1 ch. 7, Table 7.15. Fossil methane scores higher because it oxidises to CO<sub>2</sub> that wasn't in the carbon cycle before.)

So a tonne of methane counts as about 30 tonnes of CO<sub>2</sub> on the usual 100-year measure, or about 80 on a 20-year one. Neither is "right". The 100-year measure understates methane's effect on the next two decades of warming. A newer metric, GWP\*, treats a *steady* methane source very differently from a *rising* one, because a constant flow of a short-lived gas holds the temperature up rather than pushing it ever higher (Meinshausen 2022 summarises the debate). The AR6 metric discussion says the right metric depends on the purpose of the analysis.

That is the practical case for Patrick's design. If the books keep the physical quantity of each gas, any metric can be applied later, and changed, without re-measuring anything. If they keep only CO<sub>2</sub>e, the choice of metric is frozen into the record.

### "Coal does not have fugitive emissions"

Not correct. Methane is trapped in coal seams and escapes when the coal is mined: through mine ventilation, drainage systems and exposed faces, and it keeps leaking from closed mines. The IEA's *Global Methane Tracker 2026* gives 2025 figures for fossil fuel methane:

| Source | Methane, 2025 |
| --- | --- |
| Oil | 45 Mt |
| Coal | 43 Mt (39 Mt from production, about 4 Mt from inactive mines, about 1 Mt from end use) |
| Natural gas | 36 Mt |
| **Total** | **124 Mt** |

Abandoned coal mines alone released about 4.5 Mt, 60 per cent of it in China. The estimates are uncertain. One mine-by-mine study the IEA cites puts coal at 21.4 Mt, half the IEA's figure, and the energy think tank Ember estimated that most coal mine methane goes unreported in official inventories. That uncertainty is itself an argument for measuring at the mine and recording it publicly.

### "Methane, when burnt, yields more energy per molecule than straight carbon"

Correct. Burning a mole of pure carbon to CO<sub>2</sub> releases about 394 kJ. Burning a mole of methane (CH<sub>4</sub>) releases about 890 kJ, because the four hydrogen atoms burn too. Each releases one molecule of CO<sub>2</sub>, so methane gives about 2.3 times as much heat per carbon atom.

Real coal contains some hydrogen and much else, so the gap is smaller in practice. The IPCC's default combustion figures are about 56 kg of CO<sub>2</sub> per gigajoule for natural gas, about 95 for bituminous coal and about 101 for lignite (*2006 IPCC Guidelines*, vol. 2, ch. 2). Gas releases roughly 40 per cent less CO<sub>2</sub> than coal for the same heat.

Leaks eat into that advantage. On world averages, all three fossil fuels lose about a quarter of a gram of methane upstream for every megajoule delivered (*my calculation*: IEA methane figures against world consumption of roughly 165 EJ of coal, 145 EJ of gas and 195 EJ of oil). On the 100-year measure that adds about 8 per cent to coal's combustion CO<sub>2</sub> and about 13 per cent to gas's. On the 20-year measure it adds about 22 and 37 per cent. The averages hide wide differences between supply chains: the IEA's country figures for upstream intensity vary several-fold. Studies of US gas put the leak rate at which gas loses its climate advantage over coal at around 3 per cent near term, rising with the time horizon (Alvarez et al. 2012, as discussed by the Breakthrough Institute).

### "CO2e can be calculated at any point along the chain, if we retain the fuel type and quantity"

Partly correct.

- **For combustion CO<sub>2</sub>, yes.** The carbon in a fuel ends up as CO<sub>2</sub> when it burns, so fuel type and quantity fix it. "Type" has to be specific: lignite and anthracite differ, so coal should be recorded by rank, or better by its measured carbon and energy content.
- **For fugitive methane, no.** It depends on *which* mine or gas field the fuel came from, not on the fuel type. The fix is to record the methane where it is released, at the mine or the pipeline, as a quantity of methane, and pass it down the chain with the fuel.
- **For non-fuel emissions, no.** About three-quarters of global emissions come from energy; the rest come from farming, land use, industrial processes and waste (Climate Watch and WRI data, 2016 to 2021). Cement releases CO<sub>2</sub> from limestone as well as from its kiln fuel. Fertiliser releases N<sub>2</sub>O, cattle and rice paddies release methane, and clearing forest releases CO<sub>2</sub> without burning any fuel. None of this shows up in a fuel record.

So the record needs two columns, not one: **fuels burned** (type and quantity) and **gases released** directly (type and quantity). With both kept, Patrick's point holds: CO<sub>2</sub>e can be calculated at any point, by any metric.

## What has been written

### Marx and Engels: nature as a source of wealth, but not of value

The *Critique of the Gotha Programme* opens by rejecting the Programme's first sentence:

> Labour is not the source of all wealth. Nature is just as much the source of use values (and it is surely of such that material wealth consists!) as labour, which itself is only the manifestation of a force of nature, human labour power.

The [extract in this repository](../Documents/Critique_of_the_Gotha_Programme-certificates.md) starts later in Part I, so this passage isn't in it. Labour time measures what society spends of its members' lives. It says nothing about what is taken from nature. A second record is consistent with Marx's own distinction.

On the earth as a whole, *Capital* vol. 3, ch. 46:

> Even a whole society, a nation, or even all simultaneously existing societies taken together, are not the owners of the globe. They are only its possessors, its usufructuaries, and, like *boni patres familias*, they must hand it down to succeeding generations in an improved condition.

When Sergei Podolinsky tried in 1880–83 to found economics on energy, Engels answered (letter to Marx, 19 December 1882) that Podolinsky's "real discovery is that human labour has the power of detaining solar energy on the earth's surface", but that "all the economic conclusions he draws from this are wrong". Engels kept the energy account and the labour account apart. That is the shape of Patrick's proposal: two records, neither reduced to the other. How far Marx and Engels engaged with energy accounting is disputed (Burkett 2006 argues they took it more seriously than is usually said).

### The GIC: one unit of account, no calculation in kind

The *Fundamental Principles* says nothing about energy, emissions or natural limits. Nature appears only as what labour struggles against: "human labor fights against nature in order to distribute a stream of labor products over the earth using natural raw materials" ([ch. 6 a](../Documents/fundamental_principles/06_The_Social_Production_Process_in_General.md#a-production-and-reproduction)). The general social accounting deliberately records only hours: "there is no information in this 'inventory' such as so many drills, so many lathes, so many pickaxes" ([ch. 11 f](../Documents/fundamental_principles/11_Accounting_as_Summary_of_Production_and_Distribution.md#f-the-general-social-accounting)).

The GIC also argues hard against Otto Neurath's "calculation in kind", which would plan "only in sums of goods ... in kilograms, meters, tons". Its verdict: "*The Russian Revolution practically proved that production without a unit of account is madness*!", because "you can't add up kilos of iron and tons of steel" ([ch. 2 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/02_The_Social_Democratic_Revision_of_Marxism.md#d-distribution-of-means-of-production-and-consumer-goods-in-kind-as-a-bolshevik-ideal10)).

Does Patrick's proposal fall to that objection? *My reading*: no.

- **Labour time stays the only unit of account.** Certificates, the payout factor and the transfer of products all remain in hours. No one is paid or charged in carbon.
- **The fuel and gas record is a register, not a currency.** It describes a physical side of production, as a factory's logbook does. Nothing is added across it to give a price.
- **Unlike iron and steel, the gases *can* be summed for a stated purpose.** They share one physical effect, trapping heat, so science supplies conversion factors. Any such sum is a published formula applied to the retained quantities, not a hidden exchange rate.

The GIC does point to one consequence of warming that its own books would show. Its "general fund" stockpiles products for "major floods, hurricanes, peat fires, etc." and is paid for through the payout factor ([ch. 13 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#g-the-general-fund)). As disasters grow, that fund grows, and the cost of warming appears directly in everyone's hours.

### Technocracy: energy instead of labour (1930s)

In the same years as the GIC, Howard Scott's Technocracy Inc. in North America proposed "energy certificates": non-transferable credits measured in energy units, allocated to each citizen in place of money. It is the mirror image of the GIC, with energy replacing labour as the single unit. Patrick's proposal keeps both and lets neither replace the other.

### Cockshott and Cottrell, *Towards a New Socialism* (1993)

Chapter 3 considers the alternative and sets it aside: "In an industrial economy, we might reasonably price goods in terms of the energy that went into their production. If society faced overriding constraints upon the amount of energy that it could use, perhaps for environmental reasons, then this might be a good way to price things." They keep labour "because we think that society is about people, and for the moment at least, how people spend their lives remains more important than any one natural resource."

Chapter 5, "The environment and natural resources", is the strongest statement found of the position Patrick takes:

> We would argue the more radical point that ecological destruction is the result of any 'economic' decision mechanism, i.e. any decision mechanism based upon a single objective function. Any decision procedure based upon prices fails to convey information about the ecological and environmental consequences of a course of action, since these are complex and not reducible to an accounting entry.

Their conclusion: "The environmental consequences of a course of action have to be determined by scientific investigation and resolved by political struggle." Major questions should go to a referendum after open debate. The problem "cannot be reduced to a comparison of simple numbers, whether expressed in labour time or money." For farming, they propose awarding supply contracts by "a formula that takes into account both the cost (in terms of direct and indirect labour) and the environmental effects" of the chemicals and fertiliser used. That is two records weighed side by side.

### Cockshott, Cottrell and Dapprich, *Economic Planning in an Age of Climate Crisis* (2022)

The same authors, nearly thirty years later, move the other way. According to reviews (I haven't read the book itself), its last chapter proposes "a measure of value that considers all factors of production, including both labour time and emission rights", with the plan required to stay under an emissions ceiling. High-emission products are marked up: "High emission products will only be produced as long as people are willing to pay a high price for them." Dapprich's related article (*Review of Political Economy*, 2023) argues that labour values may understate environmental costs, and computes "opportunity cost valuations" by linear optimisation instead.

Renato Flores's review in *Cosmonaut* (2023) objects that this yields "a labor-carbon equivalent that completely buys into a carbon-pricing logic", that it reduces the ecological crisis to carbon, and that *Towards a New Socialism* had been right to call such trade-offs political. Flores's first objection is the one Patrick's brief anticipates ("this has nothing to do with trade"). His second applies to Patrick's proposal too: see [Open questions](#open-questions).

### Vettese and Pendergrass, *Half-Earth Socialism* (2022)

Troy Vettese and Drew Pendergrass build on Neurath directly: "total plans" in kind, with energy, land and emissions as separate physical constraints and no single metric. They set this against the integrated assessment models of mainstream climate economics, which turn everything into money. Their programme is a fully renewable energy system, rewilding half the earth to draw carbon down, and no solar geoengineering. They also made an online planning game to show the trade-offs.

### Neel and Chavez, "Forest and Factory" (*Endnotes*, 2023)

Phil Neel and Nick Chavez reject any single unit ("what we need is not one society-wide protocol but many protocols") and put ecological limits *upstream*. Limits "could be placed on the amount of fossil fuels that can be allocated to upstream uses", and on biomass burning, rice paddy area and livestock numbers. Their reason matches Patrick's choice to record fuel: it is "far simpler to account for the social and ecological impacts of energy generation" than to trace emissions through every downstream product. They propose an "atmospheric league" of climate scientists and ecological associations to balance emissions against sinks. Labour-time accounting appears only as a transitional measure to be superseded.

### Benanav, "Beyond Capitalism" (*New Left Review* 153–154, 2025)

Aaron Benanav argues against any single criterion of efficiency, whether profit or anything else, and for "multi-criteria" decisions on investment, weighing ecological limits, social needs and reductions in necessary labour time against one another. His institution is a network of investment councils. (From summaries; the articles are on his site.)

### The German labour-time accounting debate

The *Fundamental Principles* has a lively German-speaking following (see [General social work](general-social-work.md#the-current-debate-2026)). On ecology it has produced less:

- Philip Broistedt and Christian Hofmann's reader *Planwirtschaft. Staatssozialismus, Arbeitszeitrechnung, Ökologie* (Promedia, 2022) has ecology in its title. What it says on the subject wasn't found.
- In a 2023 *Jungle World* debate series on labour-time accounting, Julian Bierwirth objected that the scheme reduces "die vielfältigen stofflichen Notwendigkeiten, die bei der gesellschaftlichen Planung der Produktion zu beachten wären" ("the manifold material necessities that would have to be considered in the social planning of production") to one abstract measure. A physical record kept beside the hours answers part of that objection: *my reading*.
- A summary of another piece in the same series, "Faires Arbeiten", reports a defender arguing that labour time need not be supplemented by measures such as CO<sub>2</sub> to act against the climate crisis. I couldn't open the article, so the claim and its author are *unverified*.

### Eco-Marxism more broadly

None of these write about accounting, but they supply the historical case:

- **John Bellamy Foster**, *Marx's Ecology* (2000): Marx's "metabolic rift" between society and the soil.
- **Andreas Malm**, *Fossil Capital* (2016): British industry switched from water to coal-fired steam in the early nineteenth century not because coal was cheaper but because it let mills move to where labour could be found and controlled. Fossil fuel and the command of labour came in together.
- **Kohei Saito**, *Marx in the Anthropocene* (2023): argues for "degrowth communism".
- **Matthew Huber**, *Climate Change as Class War* (2022): argues the problem lies in production, which capital controls, not in individual consumption. The "carbon footprint" was popularised by BP's 2004 advertising campaign, with a calculator for individuals. Patrick's record is the opposite: public and attached to production.

### Existing capitalist carbon bookkeeping

Capitalist practice already does parts of this, for other ends:

- **Product carbon footprints** (ISO 14067) and the GHG Protocol's "scopes" trace emissions through supply chains. "Scope 3" is roughly what a product carries from its inputs.
- **Environmentally extended input-output analysis** (for example the EXIOBASE database) takes an economy's table of inter-industry flows and computes the emissions embodied in each product. It is the same Leontief calculation Cockshott and Cottrell use to compute labour values. One set of inter-industry flows gives embodied hours and embodied gases together.

## How the GIC's books could carry it

*This section is my reading*: a sketch, not a worked-out scheme.

### What each operational unit records

The GIC has every unit keep its production equation, (f + c) + l = product, in hours ([ch. 7 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#d-communist-reproduction)). Beside it, each unit would keep:

1. **Fuels burned in the unit**: each fuel by type and quantity, with its measured energy and carbon content. Electricity, heat and hydrogen are not fuels here; they arrive carrying the record of the fuels burned to make them.
2. **Gases released in the unit**, other than CO<sub>2</sub> from the fuels in (1): methane vented or leaked, CO<sub>2</sub> from processes such as making cement, N<sub>2</sub>O from fertiliser, methane from livestock and landfill, refrigerants. Each by gas and quantity, measured where possible.
3. **Removals**, where the unit's work takes carbon out of the air: forests, soils, capture.

### How it travels

Each product would carry two things forward: its production time, as now, and its emissions record, made up of the records its inputs arrived with plus what the unit itself burned and released. A power station passes on its coal and its share of the mine's methane with every kilowatt-hour. A courier service receives that record with its electricity.

The GIC's general social accounting already "registers the flow of products by simple 'transfer'", and the payout factor comes out of "a simple summation in the Giro Office" ([ch. 10 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor)). The emissions record is a few more columns in the same transfer. Society's total is the sum over all units, and it can be compared with the atmosphere's measured change, which is a check no capitalist inventory has to pass.

### What it decides

The record pays no one and charges no one. It informs two kinds of decision:

- **Budgets.** The council congress already sets the general social work budget in advance and caps the expansion of operational units (for example, by no more than 10 per cent of their means of production: [ch. 13 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#c-the-general-rule-for-the-expansion-of-the-operational-unit)). It could set a carbon budget the same way: so many tonnes of each fuel, so many tonnes of methane, for the year, allotted by plan as Neel and Chavez suggest. That is an allocation, not a trade.
- **Choices between methods.** Where two methods meet the same need, the record shows both costs side by side. This is where Patrick's ratio comes in.

## The ratio, worked through

### An illustration

One small parcel, a 10 km round trip across a city. **These are illustrative assumptions, not measurements**, chosen to show the shape of the books:

| | Bicycle | E-scooter, coal power | E-scooter, gas power | Petrol car |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Courier's time (assumed) | 40 min | 30 min | 30 min | 35 min (traffic, parking) |
| Fuel record | none | 3.0 MJ coal (0.3 kWh at 36% efficiency) | 2.0 MJ gas (0.3 kWh at 55% efficiency) | 0.8 L petrol |
| CO<sub>2</sub> from burning | 0 | 0.28 kg | 0.11 kg | 1.8 kg |
| Methane upstream (at 0.25 g/MJ) | 0 | 0.75 g | 0.5 g | about 6 g |
| CO<sub>2</sub>e, 100-year | 0 | 0.30 kg | 0.13 kg | 2.0 kg |
| CO<sub>2</sub>e, 20-year | 0 | 0.34 kg | 0.15 kg | 2.3 kg |

(*My calculation*, from the IPCC combustion factors and the methane averages above. The table leaves out the hours and emissions embodied in making the vehicles, roads and chargers, and the refinery's own fuel. A full GIC-style account would include them as f and c. They would add most to the car.)

The scooter's line shows Patrick's point about fuel records: the same scooter on the same trip carries two and a half times the CO<sub>2</sub> on coal power as on gas power, and almost none on wind. The courier service can't know that unless the power station's fuel record travels with the electricity.

### Which ratio to use

Taken alone, each option's ratio of hours to carbon misleads. The bicycle's is infinite, because it burns nothing. And an option with tiny figures on both sides can have the same ratio as one with large figures.

Two steps work better (*my reading*):

1. **Drop dominated options.** In this illustration the car takes more time *and* more carbon than the scooter, so it loses on both counts and needs no ratio at all. Most real choices will be like this or close to it.
2. **For the rest, compare differences.** Take the extra carbon one option costs, divided by the hours it saves. Against the bicycle:
   - scooter on coal: 0.30 kg for 10 minutes saved, about **1.8 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per hour saved**
   - scooter on gas: about **0.8 kg per hour saved**
   - car: 2.0 kg for 5 minutes saved, about **24 kg per hour saved**

A benchmark gives those figures meaning. The world currently emits about 38 Gt of fossil CO<sub>2</sub> a year for, very roughly, 7 trillion hours of paid work: about **5 kg per hour worked** (*my calculation*, order of magnitude only). A society with its own records and its own carbon budget would know its average exactly, much as the GIC's society knows its average working hour. An activity that spends far more carbon per hour saved than the social average, like the car here, is the first candidate for change. The councils still decide; the numbers only make the choice visible.

## The justification, checked

Patrick's claim of acceleration holds over the decades, though not every year. Averaged by decade, CO<sub>2</sub> at Mauna Loa has risen (*my calculation*, from NOAA's annual growth series):

| Decade | Average rise per year |
| --- | --- |
| 1960s | 0.86 ppm |
| 1970s | 1.28 ppm |
| 1980s | 1.61 ppm |
| 1990s | 1.51 ppm |
| 2000s | 1.97 ppm |
| 2010s | 2.42 ppm |
| 2020–2025 | 2.56 ppm |

The 1990s slowed slightly. The year-to-year rate swings with El Niño: 3.32 ppm in 2023 and 3.33 in 2024, the highest on record, then 2.23 ppm in 2025 (provisional). The annual mean was 354 ppm in 1990, the year of the IPCC's first report, and 427 ppm in 2025.

Fossil CO<sub>2</sub> emissions reached a record 38.1 Gt in 2025 (projected), roughly 60 per cent more than in 1990. The Global Carbon Project puts the remaining budget for a 50 per cent chance of holding warming to 1.5°C at 170 Gt of CO<sub>2</sub>: about four years at current emissions. For 2°C it is 1,055 Gt, about 25 years.

One correction of attribution: the IPCC does not make policy. It was set up in 1988 by the World Meteorological Organization and the UN Environment Programme to assess the science, and its findings have been consistent and increasingly urgent. Decisions are made by governments, under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (1992), in the annual COP meetings. The failure Patrick describes belongs to them.

## Open questions

- **Carbon is not the only limit.** Flores's objection to Cockshott, Cottrell and Dapprich applies here too: water, soil, land and biodiversity don't show up in a fuel and gas record. Should the books carry further physical records, and where does that stop before it becomes Neurath's calculation in kind?
- **Who measures fugitive emissions?** The IEA's estimate for coal methane is twice another study's. Satellites now detect large leaks, but routine measurement at every mine and field is a large job, and it is itself labour.
- **Wood and biofuel.** Burning them releases CO<sub>2</sub> that plants took up recently. Conventions treat it as neutral; critics disagree. Record it as its own fuel type and decide later.
- **Workers' food.** The European Cyclists' Federation counts about 16 g CO<sub>2</sub>e per km for the extra food a cyclist eats (2011). In the GIC's scheme a worker's consumption is not an input to the product, so counting it in the delivery would count it twice. It belongs in the consumption account. Is that right?
- **Imports from a capitalist world.** Goods arriving without a record would need estimates, for example from input-output tables.
- **Did any council communist write on this?** Pannekoek, Mattick, the later *Rätekorrespondenz*: nothing was found, but nothing was searched for specifically.
- **What did Broistedt and Hofmann's 2022 reader say on ecology?** And who wrote "Faires Arbeiten"?
- **Should the carbon budget enter the payout factor?** In this sketch it doesn't: carbon constrains what is produced, not how products are shared out. Is a budget enough without any charge on the product?
