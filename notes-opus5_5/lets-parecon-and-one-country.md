# LETS, Parecon and the GIC: what capitalist world?

Two schemes that are often put forward as alternatives to capitalist money, set beside the GIC's *Fundamental Principles*: local exchange trading systems (LETS) and participatory economics (Parecon). The question that runs through all three is what each assumes lies *outside* it. That question came from the [carbon note](carbon-and-labour-time.md#open-questions), which asked how goods "from a capitalist world" would enter the books. This note also looks at the "socialism in one country" thinking behind that question, and at Patrick's answer: the outside that matters is in time, not in space.

This is a research note by Claude (Opus 5.5), written from a brief by Patrick. Points marked *my reading* or *my calculation* are interpretation or arithmetic, not something the sources say. The Wikipedia articles Patrick linked, and marxists.org, couldn't be opened from the session this was written in. Facts about LETS and Parecon are taken from search results that quote those articles and other sources, and are cited to the sources. Quotations from Marx's *Capital* and from the GIC are taken from the [book in this repository](../Documents/fundamental_principles/README.md). Quotations marked *unchecked* were found only in secondary sources.

## Summary

- **LETS is a money; the GIC's certificate is not.** LETS is a ledger of interest-free mutual credit between members who still buy and sell. Its units are usually pegged to the national currency, and tax offices treat LETS income as income. In Marx's terms (which the GIC quotes), it is the kind of "labour money" that is still money. The GIC's certificate, like Owen's, is "no more 'money' than a ticket for the theatre" ([ch. 9 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#c-the-consumption-money)).
- **LETS is born in devastated terrain.** The first LETS was set up on Vancouver Island in 1983, when unemployment in the Comox Valley was around 25 per cent. Argentina's barter clubs peaked in the crash of 2001–02. Community currencies are what people make when money fails locally. The GIC predicted that moment for a revolution too: inflation, then a new unit of account. Whether it is a new money or the working hour "is a decisive question of power" ([ch. 17 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#b-from-the-money-account-to-the-working-time-account)).
- **Parecon is much closer to the GIC than LETS is.** It has no market and no private ownership, and it uses "socially average hours" as the basis of pay. But it pays for **effort and sacrifice**, rated by workmates, so harder or nastier work earns more. That is exactly the position of Kautsky that the GIC rejects ([ch. 7 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#e-reproduction-of-labor-power)). And it allocates by prices worked out in rounds of planning, with pollution charged a price, where the GIC transfers products at their production time.
- **Parecon assumes a capitalist world outside, and says how to trade with it.** Hahnel's "50 percent rule" gives a poorer trading partner more than half the gain from trade, "regardless of whether the trading partner is a participatory economy or a capitalist economy". That is a doctrine for coexistence. LETS assumes capitalism by design: it is "complementary to the national currency". The GIC says nothing about foreign trade at all.
- **"Socialism in one country" was Stalin's revision of Marx and Engels.** Engels answered "No" to whether the revolution could happen in one country alone (1847). Stalin wrote the opposite into *Foundations of Leninism* in 1924. In 1952 he described "two parallel world markets also confronting one another". Even then, trade inside the Soviet bloc was priced from world capitalist prices. The labour-time tradition's own one-country answer is Cockshott and Cottrell's (1993): import from the capitalist world and pay in labour credits.
- **The GIC's boundary is the accounting, not the border.** "Those who are not members of the social accounting cannot receive raw materials" ([ch. 16](../Documents/fundamental_principles/16_The_Economic_Dictatorship_of_the_Proletariat.md)). It also refuses the excuse that Russia failed because the world revolution did not come ([ch. 2 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/02_The_Social_Democratic_Revision_of_Marxism.md#d-distribution-of-means-of-production-and-consumer-goods-in-kind-as-a-bolshevik-ideal10)).
- **Patrick's temporal reading fits the GIC's own method.** The biggest store of "goods arriving without a record" is everything a revolution inherits: the factories, the stocks, and an atmosphere at 427 ppm. The GIC has a tool for the first two, the "key number" that turns money values into estimated hours ([ch. 17 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#c-the-key-number)). The atmosphere is different. Its record already exists, but no unit can pay it off; it can only be budgeted for and worked down. Genus *Homo* is about 2.8 million years old and our species about 300,000. Air like today's last existed around 3 million years ago, and possibly 14 million.

## LETS

### What it is

A LETS is a shared ledger. Members list what they offer and want. When one does work for another, the buyer's account is debited and the seller's credited, in the system's own unit. No one has to hold units before buying: an account simply goes into debit. So all the balances add up to zero, credit is created by members for members, and no interest is charged. If a member leaves in debit, the loss "is absorbed equally by all members" (sources in [LETS, search results](#sources)).

Michael Linton started the first one, the Comox Valley LETSystem in Courtenay on Vancouver Island, in early 1983, "in response to the disastrous local effects of the recession of 1982". Unemployment in the valley "hovered around 25%". He called the unit "green dollars", for the environmental and social good he expected of it. The system "was intended as complementary to the national currency, rather than a replacement for it."

The idea spread in the 1990s, most of all in Britain, where over 450 schemes with about 40,000 members were set up. Growth peaked there at the end of the 1990s, and a later count found about 250 still going. Researchers describe the 1990s wave as having "largely subsided".

Relatives:

- **Time banks** (Edgar Cahn's "Time Dollars", from the 1980s): an hour of anyone's work earns one hour of credit, whatever the work.
- **Ithaca HOURS** (Paul Glover, 1991): printed notes, one HOUR worth one hour of basic labour or $10. Millions of dollars' worth changed hands among more than 500 businesses before use declined.
- **Argentina's barter clubs** (*Red Global de Trueque*, from 1995): printed *créditos* used by perhaps 7 to 10 per cent of the population by mid-2002, in the middle of the country's financial collapse. They collapsed in turn in 2002–03, from over-issue and counterfeiting, and as the peso recovered.
- **The Australian Tax Office** treats barter and trade exchanges like cash for income tax and GST, and a business paid in LETS units "is just as taxable as if the plumber was paid in dollars" (Brisbane LETS).

### Against the GIC

LETS and the GIC's certificate both record what a person has given and what they can take. That is where the likeness ends.

- **LETS keeps commodity exchange.** Members are private producers who sell to each other. The unit measures a price agreed between them, not time worked. Marx's objection to the labour-money schemes of his day, in the *Grundrisse* against Darimon and John Gray, was that they hoped to keep commodity production while abolishing money's power. Marx summarised Darimon's aim as: "Let the pope remain, but make everybody pope. Abolish money by making every commodity money" (*unchecked*: quoted in a secondary source). LETS fits that description: every member can issue money.
- **The GIC quotes Marx on the difference.** Owen's labour money "is no more 'money' than a ticket for the theatre ... the certificate of labor is merely evidence of the part taken by the individual in the common labor, and of his right to a certain portion of the common produce destined for consumption" (*Capital* vol. 1, quoted in [ch. 9 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#c-the-consumption-money)). Owen's notes presupposed directly associated labour. A LETS unit presupposes the opposite.
- **Even time banks don't escape this.** "One hour equals one hour" sounds like the GIC's equal hour. But a time bank records hours traded between private persons, and covers only the services members choose to swap. The GIC's hour runs through *all* production, means of production included: that, it says, is what earlier schemes missed, "since *none of them includes the means of production* in their calculations, they lead nowhere" ([ch. 4](../Documents/fundamental_principles/04_Progress_in_the_Formulation_of_the_Problem.md)).

### LETS and devastated terrain

*My reading.* LETS is the case where Patrick's "devastated terrain" is literal. The Comox Valley in 1983 and Argentina in 2001 were places where national money had stopped reaching people who could still work. Their answer was to make a local money.

The GIC foresaw exactly this fork for a revolution. It expected inflation in "the first period" of any proletarian dictatorship, "whatever the course of the revolution", until "the working class is faced with the question of 'stabilization', with the introduction of a new unit of account. If the working class lacks the strength to implement communism, a new currency, a new kind of money will be created" ([ch. 17 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#b-from-the-money-account-to-the-working-time-account)). The barter clubs are that second branch: a new money, made in a collapse, by people who didn't hold the means of production. The Spanish collectives' local vouchers, in [the note on the Spanish Revolution](spanish-revolution-money.md), are another.

## Parecon

### What it is

Participatory economics was set out by Michael Albert and Robin Hahnel in two books in 1991: *Looking Forward* and *The Political Economy of Participatory Economics* (Princeton). Albert's *Parecon: Life After Capitalism* (Verso, 2003) is the fullest popular account. Hahnel's *A Participatory Economy* (AK Press, 2022) is the latest. Its institutions:

- **Workers' councils and consumers' councils**, nested from the workplace and the neighbourhood up.
- **Balanced job complexes**: each job is a bundle of tasks balanced against the others "for equity and empowerment", so that no one only gives orders and no one only takes them.
- **Pay for effort and sacrifice.** Effort is "personal sacrifice for the social endeavor": longer hours, or less pleasant, more intense or more dangerous work. Workmates rate it. Need is met separately for children, the old, the sick and those who can't work.
- **Participatory planning.** Councils propose what they want to produce and consume. A facilitation board announces "indicative prices"; councils revise their proposals; the rounds repeat until the plan balances.
- **Pollution priced inside the plan.** Hahnel's "pollution damage revealing mechanism" (*Review of Radical Political Economics*, 2017) asks affected communities to report the harm a pollutant does them. Enterprises then pay that price for each unit they emit, as for any other input, and communities that accept pollution are compensated.

### Against the GIC

Parecon and the GIC share more than either shares with LETS: no market, no private ownership of the means of production, councils, and hours as the basis of consumption. *Looking Forward* says "the number of hours a person works can serve as a rough measure of one's contribution", and that because jobs are balanced, "socially average hours" are a real, measurable quantity. Albert's "base income" is what a worker earns for thirty hours at average intensity in a balanced job.

The differences are sharp, though:

- **Unequal pay for unequal effort.** Parecon pays more for harder, longer or nastier work. The GIC takes on precisely this argument from Kautsky, who held that "*higher wages should be paid for unpleasant or heavy work than for pleasant and light work*". Its answer: that "means nothing other than that even in communism the '*struggle for better working conditions*' does not stop, that the distribution of the social product of production has an antagonistic character" ([ch. 7 e](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#e-reproduction-of-labor-power)). The GIC pays every hour the same. Parecon answers the same problem another way: by balancing the jobs, so that the nasty work is shared out rather than paid more.
- **Prices or production times.** In Parecon, products move at indicative prices that are meant to reflect their full social cost, set by rounds of negotiation. In the GIC, products move at their production time, (f + c) + l, worked out from each unit's own books, with no central body setting anything ([ch. 7](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#a-the-transfer-of-the-goods)). Parecon's iteration is a planned market in all but name, its critics say. The GIC would add that every round is a struggle between councils over shares.
- **More councils.** The GIC mocked Cole's guild socialism for solving every difficulty by setting up "a new commission or a special council" ([ch. 17 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#d-utopism)). Parecon's nested councils, job-balancing committees and facilitation boards are open to the same charge. The GIC's alternative is "the laws of economic movement": a few general rules, applied in each unit's own accounts.
- **Pollution priced, or recorded.** Parecon's pollution price is the opposite of Patrick's proposal in the [carbon note](carbon-and-labour-time.md), which keeps the fuel and gas record beside the hours and never converts one into the other. Parecon does what Renato Flores criticised Cockshott, Cottrell and Dapprich for: it makes a common measure of labour and emissions.

Libertarian critics also charge that Parecon keeps a wage relation in a new form (Steven Johns, on libcom, with a long reply from Albert). David Schweickart argues it would be undesirable even if it were feasible.

## The outside: "socialism in one country"

### Marx and Engels: no

Engels's *Principles of Communism* (1847), the draft of the *Manifesto*, asks in Question 19: "Will it be possible for this revolution to take place in one country alone?" His answer begins "No", on the ground that big industry and the world market had already bound the peoples of the earth together. *The German Ideology* (1845–46) has communism "only possible as the act of the dominant peoples 'all at once' and simultaneously".

The *Critique of the Gotha Programme* is more careful. Marx accepts that the working class must organise "at home", but objects to the Programme's claim to work "within the framework of the present-day national state": that framework, he says, is itself "economically 'within the framework' of the world market" (*unchecked*: the second phrase is quoted in a secondary source). The [extract in this repository](../Documents/Critique_of_the_Gotha_Programme-certificates.md) does not include this part.

### Stalin: yes

The first edition of Stalin's *Foundations of Leninism* (lectures of April 1924) still said that the final victory of socialism in one country needed the efforts of the proletarians of several advanced countries. Later in 1924 (sources differ between August and December) Stalin replaced that with the claim that the proletariat of one victorious country "can and must build a socialist society". It became the doctrine of his side against Trotsky.

Its economics followed:

- **A state monopoly of foreign trade** from 23 April 1918, one of the measures the GIC lists in [its appendix](../Documents/fundamental_principles/90_Appendix.md#the-course-of-nationalization-in-soviet-russia-from-november-1917-to-1921) on Soviet nationalisation.
- **Two world markets.** In *Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR* (1952), Stalin held that the most important economic result of the Second World War was that "the single all-embracing world market disintegrated, so that now we have two parallel world markets, also confronting one another" (as given in search results from the marxists.org text; William Z. Foster quotes the same words in 1953).
- **But the "socialist world market" priced from the capitalist one.** From 1958, trade within Comecon (the Soviet bloc's trading organisation) was priced by the "Bucharest formula": adjusted world market prices. From 1975 those were a moving average of the previous five years' world prices. The lag later worked in the Soviet Union's favour, when its oil kept its price in the bloc after world prices fell in the 1980s.

### The council communists: state capitalism

The GIC's answer to the Soviet Union came in the *Theses on Bolshevism* (1934, drafted by Helmut Wagner, issued under the name Rudolf Sprenger). Bolshevism was "a movement and method of the bourgeois revolution in a preponderantly peasant country", and the economy it built was state capitalism. On that view "socialism in one country" was never socialism, in one country or many.

The *Fundamental Principles* itself had already refused the isolation excuse. "It was by no means the absence of the world revolution, nor was the individual peasant enterprise the reason why the Soviet state had to abandon its plans for 'moneyless production and distribution' ... It only turned out that production and distribution on this 'communist basis' were impossible" ([ch. 2 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/02_The_Social_Democratic_Revision_of_Marxism.md#d-distribution-of-means-of-production-and-consumer-goods-in-kind-as-a-bolshevik-ideal10)). For the GIC, the failure was the missing unit of account and the state's command of labour, not the capitalist world around Russia.

### The labour-time tradition's own one-country answer

Cockshott and Cottrell's *Towards a New Socialism* (1993) is the only text in this repository that plans for foreign trade. Its ch. 10, "Foreign Trade", begins: "The socialist movement lacks a definite theory of foreign trade." It assumes "distinct nation states" and "trade with the capitalist world", and proposes (pp. 130–131):

> imports from the capitalist world are paid for in labour credits; labour credits may be exported and can circulate abroad but not at home; and the import of foreign currency is outlawed.

Foreign trade becomes "just a specialised branch of production that produces imported goods and consumes exported goods". A good is imported only if its labour price is below the hours it would take at home. Their worked example is a "Norwegian commonwealth" that exports oil for cars. On the carbon note's terms, that is a socialist economy paying its way by selling fossil fuel to capitalists (*my reading*).

They also condemn the Eastern bloc's borrowing from Western banks in the 1970s: "the supposedly socialist state is turned into an agency for world capital". Their advice is Mao's: balanced trade and no loans.

### Where LETS and Parecon stand

*My reading*:

| | What lies outside | How it deals with the outside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LETS | Capitalism, all around it | It is "socialism in one town" without the socialism: a ledger for members, inside the national economy, pegged to its money and taxed by its state |
| Parecon | Other economies, capitalist or participatory | A doctrine of coexistence: trade by the "50 percent rule", but no foreign direct investment, which "is at odds with worker self-management" |
| Stalin | The capitalist camp | A state monopoly of foreign trade, a second world market, and prices borrowed from the first |
| Cockshott and Cottrell | The capitalist world | Imports paid in labour credits, which circulate only abroad |
| GIC | Producers who stay out of the social accounting | They "exclude themselves from the communist community" and receive no raw materials. Nothing on other countries |

So the carbon note's question ("goods arriving without a record") belongs to the Cockshott and Cottrell or the Stalinist frame: a socialist economy with a frontier and a customs house. The GIC's frame has no customs house. Its outside is any producer who hasn't joined the accounts, and it expects them to join, because there's no other way to get raw materials. The GIC calls this "a dictatorship which 'dies' of its own accord" ([ch. 16](../Documents/fundamental_principles/16_The_Economic_Dictatorship_of_the_Proletariat.md)).

## What capitalist world? The outside in time

### Patrick's argument

If the revolution is international, as Marx, Engels and the GIC all assumed, there's no capitalist country to import from. But there is still something that arrives without a record: everything the new society inherits from the old one. Patrick's image is the "devastated terrain" of Gaza. The inheritance is a poisoned planet with an atmosphere humans are not adapted to.

### The facts checked

- **The atmosphere.** CO<sub>2</sub> at Mauna Loa averaged 427 ppm in 2025, up from about 280 ppm before industry (NOAA; see the [carbon note](carbon-and-labour-time.md#the-justification-checked)). Ice cores cover the last 800,000 years, and "none of the ice core record exceeds modern day values"; most of it is well below 300 ppm.
- **When air was last like this.** Patrick's "3 million years" is the usual figure: the warm Pliocene, about 3 million years ago, had CO<sub>2</sub> around 400 ppm, sea levels 10 to 40 metres higher than now, and much smaller ice sheets (phys.org on Willeit and others, 2019; Pancost). A larger review, the CenCO2PIP consortium in *Science* (December 2023), puts the last time CO<sub>2</sub> was as high as today at about **14 million years** ago. The older the estimate, the less certain it is. Both answers say the same thing for Patrick's purpose.
- **The species.** The oldest fossil of genus *Homo*, a jaw from Ledi-Geraru in Ethiopia, is 2.8 to 2.75 million years old. The oldest securely dated *Homo sapiens*, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, is about 300,000 years old. So almost the whole history of the genus has passed in the cooler, lower-CO<sub>2</sub> world that came after the Pliocene, and our species has never breathed air like today's (*my reading* of these dates).
- **Civilisation.** Farming began at about the start of the Holocene, the stable warm period that began 11,700 years ago. The first cities, such as Uruk in Mesopotamia, are about 6,000 years old. All of civilisation fits inside that one stable climate, which the present rise in CO<sub>2</sub> is pushing the planet out of. (These are standard figures, not checked for this note.)
- **"Species being"** is Marx's term (*Gattungswesen*) in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* of 1844: humanity as a species that makes its own life, and its relation to nature, through its work. Patrick's question puts it well. The species being's "inorganic body", as Marx calls nature in the same manuscripts, has been changed under it.
- **Gaza.** UNEP's second environmental assessment (23 September 2025) estimates more than 61 million tonnes of debris, twenty times the debris of all earlier conflicts in Gaza since 2008. About 15 per cent of it may be contaminated with asbestos, industrial waste or heavy metals. By May 2025, 97 per cent of tree crops had been damaged, all five wastewater treatment plants were shut, and water and sanitation systems were "almost entirely defunct". Recovery of the soil may take decades.

### What the GIC already has for the inheritance

*My reading.* The GIC did face the problem of goods without a record. Its answer was for the inheritance, not for imports. When the old money gives way, every operational unit holds stocks and machines valued in money, not hours. The GIC's answer is the "key number": take the cost accounts of mass products like coal, electricity and iron, estimate how many hours a unit of money stood for, and convert everything by that factor. "It is not possible to calculate the first key number exactly. We can only try to estimate it as accurately as possible. As soon as the calculation has been generally implemented, the actual production times are displayed very quickly" ([ch. 17 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md#c-the-key-number)).

That is the same move the carbon note proposed for imports: estimate once, from the old society's figures, then let the new books take over. Input-output tables are the modern form of the GIC's cost accounts.

The carbon side of the inheritance is different in two ways.

- **It already has a record.** The atmosphere is measured continuously. The Global Carbon Project and others reconstruct historical emissions by country and by fuel. Unlike the inherited machines, the inherited carbon does not need a key number.
- **It cannot be transferred.** The GIC's books work because products move from unit to unit and the hours move with them. The carbon already in the air moves to no one. It cannot be paid for, written off or passed down a chain. It can only be budgeted for (how much more may be released) and worked down (removals, which are labour). In the GIC's books that labour would most naturally be general social work, free to all, like the general fund the GIC sets up for "major floods, hurricanes, peat fires" ([ch. 13 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/13_The_Expansion_of_Production.md#g-the-general-fund)).

### Is the carbon note's question still needed?

Partly. *My reading*: Patrick is right that "a capitalist world" outside a communist society is the Stalinist frame, and that the larger unrecorded import is the past. But two smaller outsides remain even in the GIC's frame:

- **Producers who haven't joined.** The GIC expects "various parts of the agriculture" and some syndicalist workers to stay out at first ([ch. 16](../Documents/fundamental_principles/16_The_Economic_Dictatorship_of_the_Proletariat.md)). Whatever passes between them and the social accounting arrives without a record.
- **A revolution is not simultaneous.** Even a world revolution takes years to spread, as 1917–23 did. In the meantime there are frontiers.

For both, the key number method serves: a one-off estimate, replaced by real records as soon as they exist. Neither needs a theory of foreign trade.

## Open questions

- **Does Parecon's job balancing answer the GIC's objection to Kautsky?** If the nasty work is shared out equally, unequal pay for it may rarely be needed. Then the remaining difference is how hard people work in the same job. Should the GIC's equal hour allow for that, or is it, as the GIC says, the start of the struggle over shares?
- **Hahnel's book itself.** *A Participatory Economy* (2022) has the fullest account of trade and investment. The points here come from his earlier essays and summaries. What does it say about trade with a capitalist world in the transition, and about prices for imports?
- **Is LETS a step towards anything?** The GIC's view of the Spanish vouchers was that "the wage system is upheld" ([Spanish Revolution note](spanish-revolution-money.md)). Is a local mutual-credit ledger any better as practice for council accounting, or does it train people in exactly the wrong habits?
- **Removals as general social work.** If drawing carbon down is labour paid for by everyone through the payout factor, how big would it be? On the carbon note's figures, the inherited excess is roughly 150 ppm of CO<sub>2</sub> above pre-industrial levels.
- **Did any council communist write on foreign trade?** Nothing was found in the *Fundamental Principles*. Pannekoek's *Workers' Councils* (1946) is the obvious place to look.
