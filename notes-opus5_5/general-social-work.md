# General social work and productive work

What the GIC's split between "productive" and "public" operational units in [chapter 10](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md) actually rests on, where it has been taken up since, and three places where it gets harder today: unpaid domestic and care work, digital goods, and AI.

This is a research note by Claude (Opus 5.5), written from a conversation with Patrick. Points marked *my reading* are interpretation, not something the sources say.

## What the distinction is

The GIC draws the line at **distribution**, not production. A productive operational unit hands its output to consumers in exchange for labour certificates. A public operational unit, or "general social work unit" (GSW unit), hands it over free, "according to the principle of 'taking as needed'" ([ch. 10 a](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#a-two-forms-of-distribution)).

Four things follow from the text:

- **It is not goods vs services.** Transport companies are "productive" units even though they "do not have to supply an actual 'product' at all" ([ch. 10 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor)).
- **It is not a ranking of labour.** Every hour counts the same. Workers in both kinds of unit are in the denominator of the payout factor, the "factor of individual consumption" (FIC):

  FIC = (L − (F<sub>p</sub> + C<sub>p</sub>)) / (L + L<sub>p</sub>)

  GIC's uses the following example for the equation: (600 − 58) / (600 + 50) = 542 / 650 = 0.83. The numbers are used below when explaining the variables.

  The letters follow the production equation (f + c) + l ([ch. 7 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/07_The_Communist_Production.md#d-communist-reproduction)). Capitals are totals across all units, and the index p marks the public (GSW) units ([ch. 10 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#b-the-gsw-budget)):

  - **L**: hours worked in the productive units (600 million). After those units replace their own used means of production and raw materials, L is also what is left of the product mass.
  - **F<sub>p</sub>**: "fixed" means of production (machines, buildings) used up by the public units (8 million).
  - **C<sub>p</sub>**: "circulating" means of production (raw materials, consumables) used up by the public units (50 million).
  - **L<sub>p</sub>**: hours worked in the public units (50 million): the teachers, nurses and so on.

  So the top line is the product left for individual consumption, and the bottom line is all hours worked, in both kinds of unit.

  **Watch the letters in ch. 10 g.** The book writes the productive units' budget as "(F + P) + L = product mass" (100 million + 600 million + 600 million). P there means raw materials and consumables, the same thing it calls c (and C<sub>p</sub>) everywhere else; it has nothing to do with the index p for "public". Read it as (F + C) + L ([ch. 10 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor)).

  Teachers and nurses add nothing to the stock of consumer goods, yet they are paid like everyone else. The cost falls on "living labor power" as a whole ([ch. 10 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#g-the-payout-factor)).
- **It is meant to move.** The GSW sector is expected to grow into food supply, personal transport and housing. The payout factor "becomes smaller and smaller as communism grows" ([ch. 10 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#h-the-growth-process-of-communism)). A branch switches over when it runs "smoothly", and "all calculations in these companies remain the same".
- **Interdependence is handled by "mixed operational units".** A power station is free for households but must charge its electricity to factories as raw material. And "the transport of goods should never be included in the 'take as needed' category" ([ch. 10 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#i-mixed-operational-units)). A road would be a mixed unit: its freight use goes into product costs, its personal use is free.

So the point that public and productive units "feed into each other" doesn't trouble the scheme. Shoe factories and tanneries feed into each other too. The transfer accounting between units handles that; the GSW line only decides what reaches the final consumer free.

### Marx's version

The GSW fund is the second and third of Marx's deductions "before this is divided among the individuals" in the [Critique of the Gotha Programme](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1875/gotha/ch01.htm):

> First, the general costs of administration not belonging to production. ... Second, that which is intended for the common satisfaction of needs, such as schools, health services, etc. From the outset, this part grows considerably in comparison with present-day society, and it grows in proportion as the new society develops. Third, funds for those unable to work, etc.

The GIC says it reached the same solution before reading this text ([ch. 10 f](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#f-the-marxist-solution)). The [extract in this repository](../Documents/Critique_of_the_Gotha_Programme-certificates.md) starts after this passage.

## Where else the distinction comes up

### Cockshott and Cottrell, *Towards a New Socialism* (1993)

The closest modern parallel, and the most explicit about *where to draw the line*. Chapter 5, "Market and non-market distribution", gives four tests:

1. **Rights of citizenship.** Goods "which are basic prerequisites for full participation in the productive and communal life of the society should be provided as of right". Their examples are education, health care and child care.
2. **Freedom of choice.** Everything else (food, drink, books, clothing, holidays) is bought with labour tokens, because people should choose "between caviar, wine, books, shirts or trips to the highlands".
3. **Coping with scarcity.** A congested motorway should carry a toll even though roads are usually free. The toll rations it and tells planners whether building more road is worth the labour.
4. **Costs of metering.** There is "no point in charging people individually for a good if the costs of metering their consumption and billing them exceed the revenue". Their example is water.

The third test cuts against the GIC's expectation that the free sector only ever grows. A free good can become a charged one when it is scarce.

On funding (chapter 7, "Tax policy"), they reject the Soviet turnover tax for the same reason the GIC rejects "price policy": it "would involve systematically pricing consumer goods at values greater than their true labour content". Where the GIC takes a fixed *proportion* of every hour (the payout factor), they propose a flat tax, "so many labour tokens per month or year per earner". Their reason: "each able-bodied person of working age is obliged to perform a basic amount of work for the commonwealth". The book never mentions the GIC, Pannekoek or Mattick.

### Marx's productive and unproductive labour

This is a different distinction, often confused with the GIC's. For Marx, productive labour is labour that produces surplus value for capital. It is a category of capitalism, and the long debate about it (Gough in the 1970s; Shaikh and Tonak, *Measuring the Wealth of Nations*, 1994) has no direct purchase in the GIC's scheme, where there is no surplus value.

### The Soviet Material Product System

Soviet national accounts counted only material production, plus the services directly serving it, as national income. Health, education and administration were the "non-productive sphere". That is the goods/services line built into planning, and it made services look like a drain on the economy. The GIC's version carries no such judgement.

### Negri and operaismo

Tronti's "social factory" and Negri's later work argue that capital absorbs the whole of social life, so the productive/unproductive line dissolves. Hardt and Negri's *Empire* (2000) ends with a demand for a social wage and guaranteed income for all. That is a demand made inside capitalism. Its premise, that individual contribution can no longer be measured, is the opposite of the GIC's: the GIC's whole project is exact measurement of labour time.

## Unpaid domestic and care work

### The size of it

The ILO's 2018 report *Care work and care jobs for the future of decent work* found 16.4 billion hours a day of unpaid care work in the 64 countries surveyed. That is the equivalent of about 2 billion people working eight-hour days without pay. Women did 76.2 per cent of it. Over two decades, in the 23 countries with data, the gender gap shrank by 7 minutes a day.

### What the GIC says

Nothing directly. A search of the English text finds no mention of housework or domestic labour. The household appears only as a unit of consumption, in [ch. 9 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#i-fair-distribution):

> Take two workers, both of whom give their best to society. But one is unmarried, while the other has a family with five children. Another is married, while husband and wife both work so that they have 'double' income.

The implied third case, a couple where only one partner works in an operational unit, is the one that matters here. The other partner's work doesn't appear in L or L<sub>p</sub>. They have no certificates of their own and consume out of their partner's. Marx makes the same point about "one worker is married, another is not", and leaves it there too.

### The classic answers

- **Socialise it.** August Bebel, then Kollontai ("Communism and the Family", 1920) and Lenin. Lenin wrote in 1919 that "petty housework crushes, strangles, stultifies and degrades" women, and pointed to "public catering establishments, nurseries, kindergartens" as the "shoots" of communism. In GIC terms this means moving the work into GSW units. It fits the GIC's growth process exactly: a branch that runs smoothly switches over to "taking as needed".
- **Pay it.** The Wages for Housework campaign (Mariarosa Dalla Costa, Selma James, Silvia Federici, from 1972). Federici's *Wages Against Housework* (1975) opens: "They say it is love. We say it is unwaged work." The demand was meant to make the work visible and refusable, not to make housework a permanent job.
- **Abolish the separate sphere.** Endnotes' "The Logic of Gender" (*Endnotes* 3, 2013) splits activity into a "directly market-mediated" sphere and an "indirectly market-mediated" one. In the second there is "no impersonal mechanism or way to objectively quantify, enforce or equalise" the work, so "an 'equal and just' sharing of these activities ... must be constantly negotiated". It doesn't discuss labour-time accounting. But its argument implies that any scheme which counts some activity and not the rest recreates the split: *my reading*.
- **Communes.** Cockshott and Cottrell (chapter 1, "Women's economic subordination") call the domestic economy a mode of production in its own right, "ultimately responsible for" the inequality between women and men. Its exploitation is "cloaked as it is in the ideologies of sexual and maternal love". Their answer (chapter 12) is voluntary urban communes that take over housing, food preparation, childcare and care of the elderly. Inside a commune, "if I perform a day's work in the commune kitchens or if I perform a day's work in the national economy, I as an individual will be paid by the commune in commune work-units". Domestic work is paid at par, but only once it is collective.

### The current debate (2026)

The question has been argued out in the German-speaking circle around the 2020 re-edition of the *Fundamental Principles*:

1. **Initiative Demokratische Arbeitszeitrechnung (IDA)**, ["Feministisch vergesellschaften?"](https://arbeitszeitrechnung.org/die-chance-der-arbeitszeitrechnung-fuer-das-problem-der-privaten-reproduktionsarbeit/) (1 February 2026). Private care work should earn labour certificates, either for recorded hours or as agreed flat rates per task. Within the model's logic it "would belong to the public sector", since nobody pays certificates to receive it. They accept that a bigger public sector means a smaller payout factor: "Je größer der öffentliche Sektor, desto kleiner der Stundenwert des Arbeitszertifikates" ("the larger the public sector, the smaller the hourly value of a labour certificate").
2. **Hermann Lueer**, the translator of the English text in this repository, ["Gesellschaftliche versus private Reproduktionsarbeit"](https://arbeiterstimmen.wordpress.com/2026/02/07/gesellschaftliche-versus-private-reproduktionsarbeit/) (dated 4 February 2026). Certificates "sind folglich keine Entlohnung für beliebige Tätigkeiten" ("are therefore not payment for arbitrary activities"). Paying for private care means "dieselbe Arbeitszeit zweifach wirksam" ("the same labour time takes effect twice"). Claims Z would exceed products P, a "reiner Rechenfehler, der zwangsläufig zu Mangel führt" ("a pure accounting error that inevitably leads to scarcity"). His alternative is either real socialisation (kindergartens, care homes, communal kitchens) or care that "bleibt privat und begründet keine individuellen Entnahmeansprüche" ("stays private and grounds no individual claims").
3. **Fredo Corvo**, ["Drei Beiträge zur Sozialisierung der privaten Haus- und Sorgearbeit"](https://arbeiterstimmen.wordpress.com/2026/02/07/drei-beitrage-zur-sozialisierung-der-privaten-haus-und-sorgearbeit/) (7 February 2026; [English](https://leftdis.wordpress.com/2026/02/07/three-contributions-to-the-socialization-of-private-domestic-and-care-work/)). Sides with Lueer. He treats the Soviet communal kitchens as emergency measures rather than communism, and proposes that a council power "should offer further socialisation of domestic and care work on a voluntary basis".
4. **IDA's reply**, ["Private Reproduktionsarbeit ist gesellschaftliche Arbeit!"](https://arbeitszeitrechnung.org/private-reproduktionsarbeit-ist-gesellschaftliche-arbeit/) (24 April 2026). Hospital staff also get certificates while adding no goods to the consumption fund ("Auch das Personal eines Krankenhauses bekommt Zertifikate, leistet aber sehr wohl Dienste"), and the payout factor exists for exactly that. They concede that the work "bliebe faktisch privat" ("would in fact stay private"), without planning or collective control.

### Assessment (*my reading*)

On the arithmetic, IDA is right and Lueer is wrong. Paid home care is formally the same as L<sub>p</sub>. Using the GIC's own figures, credit 100 million hours of home care as public labour with no means of production:

FIC = 542 / (650 + 100) = 0.72

Everyone's 40 hours now buy 28.9 hours of products instead of 33.2. Total claims are 0.7227 × 750 = 542 million hours, exactly the product mass. Nothing is counted twice. The change moves consumption from households that do less care work to households that do more, and makes the amount a public decision.

Lueer's real point lies elsewhere, and it holds. A GSW unit is more than a line in the formula:

- **It has a budget society sets in advance.** The GIC's "108 million working hours" is what "the society wants to make available to the public operational units for the current year" ([ch. 10 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#b-the-gsw-budget)). Private care has no such limit.
- **Its hours are recorded by an operational unit under its own council.** Private care hours would be self-reported, by the person who is paid for them.
- **Its output is open to anyone.** A kindergarten serves the district; a parent serves their own child.

So the disagreement is about control and measurement, not arithmetic. The GIC's framework points to a middle route: make care work collective enough to have its own operational unit, budget and books. That is close to Cockshott and Cottrell's commune, where kitchen work and factory work are paid at par. Care that stays fully private stays outside the accounts. The GIC does say plainly that the remaining unequal measure falls hardest on the people with the most needs ([ch. 9 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#i-fair-distribution)).

Endnotes' objection remains: some care can't be timed. A night with a sick child has no average production time. A flat rate per dependant, set in the GSW budget, would avoid timing it, at the cost of no longer measuring labour.

## Digital goods

Software and digital media cost labour to make and almost nothing to copy. In GIC terms, a program's production time (f + c + l) is spent once. The production time of each copy is that total divided by the number of copies, which is unbounded and unknown in advance. So the GIC's rule that a product passes to the consumer at its average production time gives a figure close to zero, and charging certificates for copies would recover nothing worth the cost of metering (Cockshott and Cottrell's fourth test).

The natural place for digital goods is therefore the GSW sector: budget the development work and distribute the result free. *My reading*: the GIC's "taking as needed" is already reached here for anything that can be copied.

What does cost ongoing labour is the infrastructure: networks, datacentres, chips, electricity. These serve both production and private use, so they are mixed operational units on a large scale. Under §i, their use by factories and offices must be charged as raw material, and their household use can be free. The accounting problem of digital goods is apportioning infrastructure between those two uses, not pricing copies.

## AI and the falling of value

Patrick's remark: an LLM destroys value faster than anything he has seen, but human creativity, work hours, basic necessities and love have not changed.

In Marx's terms that is accurate. The value of a commodity is the labour time *currently* needed to reproduce it, not the time originally spent. When a new technique cuts that time, the value of every product of the kind falls, including what is already made, and the skills that made it are devalued too. Marx calls the equivalent effect on machines "moral depreciation" (*Capital* vol. 1, ch. 15). Under capitalism this shows up as devaluation: falling wages for writers, translators and programmers; capital written off; crisis.

In the GIC's accounting the same event looks different. A labour certificate measures hours worked, not the value of what is made. If a report takes one hour instead of ten, its production time falls to one hour. The worker's 40 hours still claim 40 × FIC hours of products, and those hours now buy more. Nobody's claim is devalued. The unit of account is the human hour, which, as Patrick says, has not changed.

Two cautions (*my reading*):

- **The machines cost labour.** Training and running models uses chips, datacentres and electricity: large F and C in GIC terms. Whether a model really saves labour is an accounting question the GIC's books would answer directly: the hours saved against the hours embodied in the infrastructure.
- **The training data is unrecorded labour.** Models are trained on the written and creative work of millions of people, much of it unpaid. In the GIC's books, as in capitalism's, that work enters as a free input, just as housework does. Both are cases of necessary labour that the accounts don't see.
