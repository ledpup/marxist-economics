# Reception of the *Fundamental Principles*

How the *[Fundamental Principles of Communist Production and Distribution](../Documents/fundamental_principles/README.md)* (German 1930, Dutch 1935) was answered, and by whom. In short: none of the authors the GIC criticises seems to have replied. The responses came from the small groups of the communist left around the GIC, and then from the reprints of the 1970s onward.

This is a research note compiled from secondary sources (listed at the end), not a finished account. Points marked *unverified* need checking against the originals.

## Did the quoted authors respond?

No reply has been found from any of them. In 1930 most were alive and active: only Marx, Engels, Wilhelm Liebknecht, Lenin and Parvus were dead (see [the list of sources](../fundamental_principles-sources/README.md)). Likely reasons:

- **Circulation was tiny.** The German edition came from the AAUD's Neuer Arbeiter-Verlag in Berlin. The GIC says it "was confiscated and destroyed" ([Foreword to the second edition](../Documents/fundamental_principles/00d_Foreword_to_the_Second_Edition.md)). The Nazis broke up the AAUD milieu in 1933.
- **The GIC was invisible to its targets.** Social Democrats and the Comintern had no reason to answer a few dozen Dutch and German council communists.
- **Events overtook them.** Tomsky, Zinoviev, Kritsman and Larin were dead or purged by 1937; Trotsky was in exile; Hilferding died in Gestapo custody in 1941; Neurath was in exile from 1934.

**Otto Leichter**, the main target ([16 quotations](../fundamental_principles-sources/leichter.md), above all in [ch. 8 b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/08_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Production.md#b-leichters-answer) and [ch. 9 d](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#d-the-consumption-money-by-leichter)), lived until 1973 but shows no sign of having answered. After 1930 his writing was about Austrian politics: *Die Sprengung des Kapitalismus* (1932), then underground work for the Revolutionary Socialists after 1934, books under pseudonyms (*Ein Staat stirbt*, 1938), exile in Paris (1938) and New York (1940), and journalism after the war. Many of his 1930s pieces were unsigned. The place to check properly is the biography by Christian Fleck and Heinrich Berger, *Gefesselt vom Sozialismus. Der Austromarxist Otto Leichter (1897–1973)* (2000).

## Responses in the 1930s

### The GIC's own account (1935)

The [Foreword to the second edition](../Documents/fundamental_principles/00d_Foreword_to_the_Second_Edition.md) is the earliest evidence that the book was discussed at all:

> The discussion about the first edition of these "Fundamental Principles" has shown that this book is often seen as a kind of "plan," which must be propagated diligently until the masses have become familiar with the economic organization based on working hours.

It also says a summary appeared in German in *Kampfsignal* (New York) and in English in *Council Correspondence* (Chicago), and that the second edition expands the point that "the same right to consumer goods is practically an unequal right".

### Paul Mattick and the American council communists (1934–1938)

Mattick, in Chicago, carried the GIC's ideas into English.

- "What is Communism?", *International Council Correspondence* no. 1 (October 1934). Probably the English summary the 1935 foreword mentions *(unverified)*.
- "Communist production and distribution", *Living Marxism* vol. 4 no. 4 (1938). Largely the same text. It argues that "the labor time method is unquestionably adapted to compute the total cost of an enterprise", and that labour money "is valid only for individual consumption". It refers readers to the *Grundprinzipien* "for a more extensive study". This is an endorsement, not a critique.

### Bilan: Hennaut and Mitchell (1935–1937)

The most substantial contemporary response came from *Bilan*, the journal of the Italian communist left in exile in Brussels, through two members of the Belgian Ligue des Communistes Internationalistes (LCI).

- **Adhémar Hennaut** summarised the book in *Bilan* nos. 19–22 (1935) under the title *Les fondements de la production et de la distribution communistes*. He was broadly sympathetic, but had reservations. He thought a party would still be needed after the revolution, and he found the book's view of the state "to say the least equivocal" and its picture of the conditions of revolution "rose-tinted".
- **Ottorino Perrone ("Vercesi")** answered at once in *Bilan* no. 21. He argued that the foundations of a communist economy can exist only on a world scale. He also argued that the law of value would still operate in a proletarian state that cannot establish communism at a stroke, so the GIC "and with them comrade Hennaut" were mistaken.
- **Jehan van den Hoven ("Mitchell")** wrote the series *Problèmes de la période de transition* in *Bilan* nos. 28, 31, 35, 37 and 38 (February 1936 to early 1937). Much of it is a polemic against what *Bilan* called "the Dutch internationalists":
  - The GIC misjudged the Russian revolution, putting its degeneration down to economic organisation rather than to its isolation as a national revolution (no. 35).
  - Their scheme, without "the law of value, the market and money", presupposes an "ideal" distribution of products (no. 35). Equality in distribution would be impossible early in the transition, especially in civil war.
  - Making economic life regulate itself "through average social labour time" leaves no room for political direction or for struggle inside the workers' organs (no. 37).
  - The proletariat must put up with a state until classes disappear, and the GIC wrongly runs together the state and the dictatorship of the proletariat (no. 37).

  The ICC says: "To the best of our knowledge, the GIC did not respond to any of these articles."

### Anton Pannekoek

Pannekoek, the GIC's closest theoretical ally, was at first sceptical. According to Bourrinet, citing Pannekoek's memoirs (*Herinneringen*, 1944), he found the planning approach "somewhat utopian and unreal". After reading the text he judged that it "gained in being known". His *Workers' Councils* (written during the war, published 1946) adopts the labour-time basis ("the fundamental data in the new production system are working hours"), but more cautiously. Bourrinet reads it as quietly criticising the GIC on scarcity and on how consumer goods are shared out. One account credits Jan Appel with persuading Pannekoek that public, communally kept books open to all could keep the system transparent *(unverified)*.

## Later reception

- **1970 reprints.** In German from Rüdiger Blankertz Verlag (Berlin), with an introduction by Mattick. In Dutch from De Vlam (Spartacusbond, Amsterdam).
  - Mattick's 1970 introduction is now more critical. Marx called the defects of distribution by labour time "unavoidable in the first phase", but did not make them a communist principle; the GIC goes further than Marx here. Calculations of social labour time are always approximate and out of date.
  - Still, he calls the book "a first attempt" and "the starting point of all serious discussion" (*der Ausgangspunkt aller ernsthaften Diskussionen*) of how to realise communist society.
- **1990 English translation** by Mike Baker (Movement for Workers' Councils, London), with Mattick's introduction and Baker's long commentary.
- **Philippe Bourrinet**, in his history of the Dutch-German communist left. He argues that labour-time accounting could bring back the law of value by giving labour time an accounting rather than a social value. He also argues that a "neutral" accounting centre could turn into a bureaucracy, and that Marx's measure of wealth under communism is free time, not labour time.
- **Gilles Dauvé** treats it as the central council-communist text on the subject, and criticises it, with Pannekoek's *Workers' Councils*, for reducing communism to democratic bookkeeping that keeps the logic of value.
- **The ICC** discusses the Bilan–GIC debate in its series *Communism is not a nice idea*, vol. 3 (2010s).
- **2020 English translation** by Hermann Lueer (the text in this repository), and a German re-edition. Around these: the [arbeitszeitrechnung.org](https://arbeitszeitrechnung.org/) group, reviews in *ak analyse & kritik* and LabourNet, and English essays in *Brooklyn Rail* (2018), workers.coop, and by Jasper Bernes.

## Open questions

- Did Leichter, Kautsky or Neurath ever mention the GIC in print? Check the Fleck and Berger biography, and *Der Kampf* (the Austrian SPÖ's theoretical monthly) for 1930–34.
- What did the "discussion about the first edition" that the 1935 foreword mentions consist of? Probably the GIC's own *Persdienst* (PIC) and the AAUD press.
- The full text of Hennaut's summary and Mitchell's series, from the *Bilan* originals.
- Did the GIC ever reply to *Bilan*, even indirectly, for example in *Rätekorrespondenz* or PIC?
