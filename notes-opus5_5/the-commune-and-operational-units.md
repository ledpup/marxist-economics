# The commune and the GIC's operational units

A summary of chapter 12 of Cockshott and Cottrell's *Towards a New Socialism* (TNS), "The Commune", set beside the kinds of operational unit in the GIC's *Fundamental Principles*. It then follows Patrick's objection: the commune's work-units may not convert one for one into the outside economy's labour tickets, and because women do most care work, a commune can become a way to devalue it.

This is a research note by Claude, written from a brief by Patrick. Points marked *my reading* or *my illustration* are interpretation or construction, not something the sources say.

## Patrick's prompt

> TNS: read chapter 12, the commune. Create a document that summarises it and compares it to the GIC, specifically the types of operational units. In my opinion, the biggest weakness of this idea is the possibility of vouchers not transferring 1:1 with vouchers outside the commune (TNS say national vouchers, but I haven't accepted socialism in one state. Logically it's fine, but at a practical level, I'm not sure how it could be achieved). As already found, women do 75–80% of primary care for children, disabled and elderly people; if vouchers do not transfer at their value, a commune can be a way to devalue women's work.

## Summary

- **The TNS commune is a household of 50 to 200 adults** that takes over five jobs of the family: housing, cooking, child care, some leisure, and helping old people. Its case is economies of scale. It is voluntary.
- **It keeps its own books in its own unit.** Members are paid in commune work-units for all their hours, inside the commune or outside it. The commune, not the member, receives the national labour tickets for outside work. Work-units can be spent in the commune or converted into national tickets "at the going exchange rate".
- **TNS say plainly that the rate can fall below one for one.** It holds at par only if the commune keeps correct accounts, balances its collective fund, and runs exactly as much "non-basic" internal work as members want to buy. Their example of failure gives 1.2 work-units per national ticket.
- **The GIC has no unit of this kind.** Every operational unit is a workplace with books in hours: productive (sells for certificates), public or GSW (free), or mixed (free to households, charged to other units). Workers are credited by society, through the giro office, hour for hour. There is one unit of account, so there is no exchange rate to fall. The GIC's nearest thing to the commune is a district that sets up its own free services and pays for them through a local payout factor ([10 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#h-the-growth-process-of-communism)).
- **Patrick's objection holds, and the chapter gives it more ground than he claims.** TNS allow four routes by which commune work, which will mostly be care work done by women, ends up worth less than outside work (*my reading*). The sharpest: TNS say that "either system can be used" for national tickets. In one, outside workers get national tickets directly and only inside workers are paid in the currency that can be devalued.
- **The problem doesn't depend on a nation.** An exchange rate appears wherever there are two sets of books. TNS's commune and foreign trade (ch. 10–11) have the same shape: an inner unit and an outer one. Patrick's doubt about "national" tickets is a doubt about the outer level. The way out in GIC terms is to give the commune no unit of its own: its kitchens, nursery and care are operational units in the social books, and their workers are credited like anyone else's.

## What chapter 12 says

### Why communes

TNS start from the loss of community in cities: "not communities but just residential areas". Socialism "needs community roots", and community "needs real economic support". Their model is China's people's communes, "the greatest cooperative experiment in history", which replaced the domestic economy in agriculture and brought "a marked advance in the social position of women". Since few people now farm, the modern version must be urban ([ch. 12](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md)).

### What a commune does

"The main function of communes is to replace the family." They list five activities ([The activities of urban communes](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#the-activities-of-urban-communes)):

| Activity | What TNS propose |
| --- | --- |
| Housing | One communal house, with at least one private room for each adult (and each "post-pubertal" member), plus more shared space than a family house has. A new architecture, after Danish co-housing. |
| Food preparation | Communal kitchens and a refectory. Cooks either full-time and paid, or on a rota. Daily bread becomes economic at 100 people. |
| Child care | Large enough for a kindergarten and perhaps a primary school. It "will free a significant percentage of the female population from individual child rearing, an activity with low labour productivity". |
| Some leisure | A pool, a gym, ponies, a boat, a country house, minibuses and a car pool. |
| Helping senior citizens | Shared cooking, cleaning and laundry, and young adults in the building, without "the stigma and isolation" of a home. |

They are firm that "we do not envisage compulsory collectivisation". Communes "will flourish only if they can demonstrate their desirability".

### Why it is efficient

"The basic rationale for communes is that they reap economies of scale." Cooking for 50 takes less labour than 20 households cooking for themselves. Shared equipment (toilets, phone lines, computers) serves more people with less. If communal child care took *more* hours than family care, "one would need other very compelling reasons to justify it". The size is set by the "minimum economic size": the economies run out at about a couple of hundred, and smaller groups are easier to run democratically ([Basic rationale](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#basic-rationale-in-terms-of-efficiency)).

### How members are paid

This is the economic core ([Systems of payment and external trade](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#systems-of-payment-and-external-trade)). Chinese communes paid in work-points, "internal units of account maintained by the commune", which gave a claim on the commune's harvest but not on national resources. TNS carry the principle over.

Work is either **internal** (for the commune) or **external** (for the national economy). For external work, "in principle either system can be used":

1. **Individuals receive national labour tickets**, and the commune charges them a membership fee.
2. **The commune receives the tickets**, like a subcontractor, and pays every member in work-units.

TNS prefer the second, because "it puts internal and external labour on a par": a day in the kitchens and a day in a factory both earn a day's work-units.

The commune's accounts then run as in their Table 12.1:

| Step | Hours |
| --- | --- |
| External labour credits (national tickets received) | 3,000 |
| less national tax | 1,100 |
| less collective purchases of national goods | 900 |
| **Distribution fund** (national tickets left for members) | **1,000** |
| Work-units credited: external 3,000 + basic internal 1,800 + non-basic internal 1,200 | 6,000 |
| less collective fund (tax + purchases + basic internal work) | 3,800 |
| **Disposable work-units** | **2,200** |

"Basic" internal work is work whose product members get as of right (cleaning, maintenance, care of non-producing members). "Non-basic" work is sold to members for work-units (meals, haircuts). Communard Jane works 36 hours, is assessed 12 for the collective fund, and has 24 work-units to spend in the commune or convert. Work-units can't be saved; to save, a member must convert ([note 2](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#systems-of-payment-and-external-trade)).

### When the rate falls

Disposable work-units (2,200) always equal the distribution fund (1,000) plus non-basic internal work (1,200), as long as the collective fund balances. If members spend 1,200 work-units on meals and haircuts, they want exactly 1,000 national tickets, and par holds. TNS's three conditions for par:

> the commune must do its accounts correctly; it must run a balanced collective fund (on average); and it must budget for just as much non-basic internal labour as commune members demand.

If they fail, "the commune will have to 'devalue' the work-unit": with a fund of 1,000 national hours and demand for 1,200, the rate is 1.2 work-units per ticket. The cause may be accounting (meals priced too low) or real: "too much labour is engaged in the commune's kitchens relative to the demand for commune meals". The remedy is "a real redistribution of the communards' labour time", and the commune "should not" act as "employer of last resort".

TNS add that a commune could choose to pay a premium for external work (1.1 work-units an hour, with a matching rate), but "since one of the objects of the commune is to break down the sexual division of labour and help establish a presumption of human equality, we would not recommend such a policy; we merely note it as a possibility".

### Tasks, legal status, policy

- **Tasks** ([Distribution of tasks](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#distribution-of-tasks)): either permanent posts (full-time cooks, child-minders) or rotation. Rotation "reduces the risk of people being typecast into sexually determined roles", but radical rotation would hurt the national economy, whose projects need people to turn up.
- **Legal status** ([The legal status of communes](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#the-legal-status-of-communes)): a commune may own its buildings and domestic means of production, rents land from the public land agency, contracts to supply labour, and may run whole projects (a milk round) with equipment lent by the national economy.
- **Public policy** ([Public policy](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/12_The_Commune.md#public-policy)): a "marriage bureau" to bring members together, public housing and credit, and perhaps tax policy favouring communes for a transitional period.

## The GIC's operational units

The GIC's basic cell is the operational unit (*Betrieb*): a workplace that keeps its own books on (f + c) + l and passes its product on ([economic model note](fundamental-principles-economic-model.md#operational-unit-and-its-family)). The book distinguishes units by how their product reaches consumers:

| Type | What it does | Example | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Productive unit** | Hands its product to consumers for labour certificates, at its production time | A shoe factory; transport, though it makes no "product" | [10 a](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#a-two-forms-of-distribution) |
| **Public unit (GSW unit)** | Works "free of charge"; its product is taken "according to the needs". Paid for from the GSW budget, set in advance, through the payout factor | Councils, the accounting office, health care, education, parks | [10 a–b](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#b-the-gsw-budget) |
| **Mixed unit** | Free to households, charged to other units as raw material | A power station | [10 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#i-mixed-operational-units) |
| **Local public unit** | A public unit of "more local significance", paid for by its district through a lower local payout factor, "the effect of a 'local tax'" | A district's public reading rooms | [10 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#h-the-growth-process-of-communism) |

Two other bodies sit beside them:

- **Cooperatives** are the consumers' organisations: "distribution is carried out collectively by *all kinds of cooperatives*", which pass consumers' wishes directly to production ([12 g](../Documents/fundamental_principles/12_The_Abolition_of_the_Market.md#g-the-cooperatives-and-the-benchmark-of-needs)). They keep no unit of their own.
- **Farms** are operational units like any other, "small or large scale … industrial or agricultural", and join by entering the social accounting ([ch. 16](../Documents/fundamental_principles/16_The_Economic_Dictatorship_of_the_Proletariat.md)). The GIC treat the organisation of farmers "with the greatest restraint" ([17](../Documents/fundamental_principles/17_Final_Considerations.md)).

The GIC expect public units to grow: once a branch runs "smoothly", food supply, personal transport and "housing service" can move to taking as needed, and "all calculations in these companies remain the same" ([10 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#h-the-growth-process-of-communism)). That list overlaps the commune's.

**How a worker is paid.** In every type, the worker receives a certificate "from society" for the hours given, less the deduction for common funds, which the payout factor makes ([9 c](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#c-the-consumption-money)). A unit is not paid an income and passes none on. A worker in a GSW unit is credited exactly as a worker in a factory is: both are in the denominator of the payout factor, L + L<sub>p</sub>.

The GIC has no household unit. The family appears only as the place where the equal hour becomes "a great injustice in practical consumption": one worker is single, another has five children ([9 i](../Documents/fundamental_principles/09_Average_Working_Hour_as_Basis_of_Consumption.md#i-fair-distribution)). Unpaid care at home is outside its books ([General social work](general-social-work.md#what-the-gic-says)).

## The two compared

| | TNS commune | GIC operational units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A household of 50–200 adults; a place to live | A workplace; nobody lives in it |
| Organised around | Consumption and reproduction (housing, meals, care) | One partial activity in the chain of production |
| Unit of account | Its own work-units, beside national labour tickets | The social hour only |
| Who is credited for outside work | The commune (TNS's preference) or the member | — (no outside) |
| Who is credited for care work | The commune, in work-units | Society, in certificates, if the care is in a public unit |
| Is internal work in the social books? | No. The nation sees external hours and taxes them; internal hours are the commune's own affair | Yes. Every unit's (f + c) + l is in the general accounting |
| Free provision | "Basic" internal work, paid for by the members alone through the collective fund | Public units, paid for by everyone through the payout factor; local extras by the district |
| Sold provision | "Non-basic" work, sold for work-units at prices the commune sets | Productive units, sold for certificates at production time |
| Can its unit lose value? | Yes; TNS work out the rate | No; there is one unit |
| Property | Owns buildings and domestic means of production; contracts with public bodies | Owns nothing; means of production are society's |
| Purpose beyond economics | Community, democracy, ending the sexual division of labour | Workers' control of production; "fair" is not claimed |

### The commune in GIC terms (*my reading*)

Every activity of the commune has a GIC home without a commune currency:

| Commune activity | GIC unit | Who pays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Child care, kindergarten, primary school | Public unit (education) | Everyone, through the payout factor |
| Helping old people, care of disabled members | Public unit (health care) | Everyone |
| Cleaning and laundry for those who need it | Public unit, or a productive unit charging production time | Everyone, or the user |
| Kitchens and refectory | Productive unit selling meals at production time; later a public unit ("food supply", 10 h) | The diner, later everyone |
| Housing | Productive unit charging the building's production time; later public ("housing service", 10 h) | The resident, later everyone |
| Pool, gym, boat, country house | Local public unit | The district (or the commune as a district) through a local payout factor |

The commune then becomes what the GIC would call a complex of operations under one roof, and a consumers' cooperative for what its members order. This is what the simulation already decided for care: a public child care unit and a public care unit record carers' hours, within a budget set in advance, and those hours are in L<sub>p</sub> for everyone ([gaps 8 and 53](../plans/simulator/gaps-decided.md#the-economics)).

## Patrick's objection: when work-units don't convert at par

### Why the risk is real

TNS's proof of par is an identity: if the collective fund balances and members buy all the non-basic work, demand for national tickets equals supply. It is sound. But each condition is a decision that 50 to 200 people make in their own books, in every commune, every period:

- **Correct accounts.** Many small sets of books, kept by amateurs, priced by the commune itself.
- **A balanced collective fund.** It carries tax, collective purchases and all basic internal work, including the care of children and old people.
- **Non-basic work matched to demand.** If members cook more meals than they buy, or the commune keeps people in its kitchens because they have nowhere else to go, the rate falls.

In the GIC's books none of these can move the value of anyone's hour, because no one's hour passes through a second unit.

### Four routes by which care work loses value (*my reading*)

**1. The individual-ticket system.** TNS allow either system. Under the first, outside workers receive national tickets directly and pay a fee; inside workers are paid in work-units. Then only inside workers hold the currency that can fall. *My illustration*: the rate is 1.2. A member who works 36 hours in a factory receives 36 national hours (before tax and fee). A member who works 36 hours in the commune nursery receives 36 work-units, which convert to 30 national hours. Her hour is worth 0.83 of his outside the commune. If nursery workers are mostly women, as care workers are now, that is a pay gap built into the accounts.

**2. The premium, by vote.** TNS "would not recommend" paying more for external work, but they leave it to each commune. A majority who work outside can vote that their work is "less attractive" and deserves 1.1 work-units an hour. TNS give the reason against it themselves: it entrenches the sexual division of labour. Nothing in their scheme stops it.

**3. The remedy falls on inside workers.** When the rate falls because "too much labour is engaged in the commune's kitchens", TNS's answer is to move labour out, and the commune must not be an "employer of last resort". Outside work is the measure; inside work is what gets adjusted. Under TNS's preferred system everyone's work-units fall together, but exit is not equal: an outside worker can leave and keep earning national tickets at par, while a full-time child-minder's job exists only inside the commune. Those with least exit bear most of the risk. TNS's own trade-off pushes toward that: rotation breaks down sex roles, but "radical" rotation hurts the national economy, so the pressure is toward full-time cooks and child-minders.

**4. Care is paid for locally.** Basic internal work (care of "non-producing members") is charged to the commune's collective fund, so its members alone pay for it. A commune with many children, old or disabled members has a larger fund, fewer disposable work-units, and less to convert. Elsewhere TNS pay for "socialised child-care" out of general taxation ([TNS model note](towards-a-new-socialism-economic-model.md#child-care)); inside the commune the book doesn't say who pays. The GIC's local payout factor has the same weakness, but the GIC keep "general needs" such as health and education in the society-wide GSW budget and leave only extras like reading rooms to the district ([10 h](../Documents/fundamental_principles/10_The_General_Social_Work.md#h-the-growth-process-of-communism)).

Behind all four: the commune's internal hours are not in the national books. The nation sees the commune as a supplier of external labour and taxes that. Care moves out of the family into a larger household, but it is still outside the social accounting, which is where [The outside and the edges](the-outside-and-the-edges.md#domestic-labour) found domestic labour in the first place.

### The scale

Women do 76.2 % of the hours of unpaid care work worldwide, and 80 % in Asia and the Pacific ([ILO, 2018](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-calls-urgent-action-prevent-looming-global-care-crisis); see [General social work](general-social-work.md#the-size-of-it)). Patrick's 75–80 % fits. TNS's own text assumes the same division: communal child care will free "a significant percentage of the female population".

### The precedent TNS cite

The Chinese work-point system that TNS take as their model did pay women less. Women were commonly awarded fewer points per day than men for the same work. Joan Hinton, who lived through it, tells of a woman whose daily points were cut from 10 to 8 when she married, and of the women of one village winning equal points only after beating the men in a rice-planting contest ([Hinton, "How Can Socialism Ensure the Full Liberation of Women?", 1997](https://michaelharrison.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/How-Can-Socialism-Ensure-The-Full-Liberation-Of-Women-Joan-Hinton-1997.pdf)). Studies of brigades also find women earning fewer points over a working life, through lighter, lower-rated work, time off for child-rearing and housework, and earlier retirement ([*Asia-Pacific Journal*, "Women and China's Socialist Construction, 1949–78"](https://apjjf.org/?p=19683)). TNS credit the communes with "a marked advance in the social position of women", which is also true; the point is that an internal unit, rated by the community, is where the old valuation came back in. (These are individual accounts and local studies; national figures are *not checked*.)

## "National" tickets and socialism in one country

TNS's chapter assumes a nation with labour tickets of its own, as their chapters on foreign trade do: labour credits "can circulate abroad but not at home" ([ch. 10](../Documents/towards_a_new_socialism/10_Foreign_Trade.md#alternatives-to-foreign-exchange); see [LETS, Parecon and the GIC](lets-parecon-and-one-country.md#the-labour-time-traditions-own-one-country-answer)). Patrick doesn't accept socialism in one state.

*My reading*: the commune's problem doesn't need a nation. It arises at any boundary between two sets of books: commune and nation, nation and world, or commune and a world federation of councils. Wherever an inner unit has to be converted into an outer one, the rate can drift, and whoever is paid only in the inner unit carries the drift. TNS's design is the same at both levels: the commune is to the nation what the nation is to the capitalist world.

So Patrick's practical doubt can be answered by removing the inner unit, not by making the conversion work. If the outer level is one set of social books, as the [payout factor at scale](payout-factor-at-scale.md) note found it can be (regions combine exactly by reporting two sums), the commune needs no unit of its own. Its nursery, kitchen and care rota are operational units, their workers are credited by the giro office hour for hour, and local extras go on a local payout factor that applies to everyone in the district alike. Nothing has to "transfer", because nothing was ever in a separate currency.

What that costs TNS: the commune loses its own pricing of meals and haircuts, and its standing as a property-holding body in "hierarchies of communally owned property". What it keeps: the housing, the shared kitchens, the economies of scale, the community, and the rotation of tasks. Those don't depend on a currency.

## What TNS get right

- **Par is the principle.** TNS want internal and external hours equal, and say why: the commune exists partly to end the sexual division of labour. Patrick's objection is to what their scheme permits, not to what they intend.
- **They name the risk.** The exchange-rate section is honest arithmetic, with the conditions for failure spelled out. The GIC never face the question because they never bring the household into the books.
- **Care is paid at all.** In the GIC, care at home is unpaid unless it becomes a public unit, and the book never says so; TNS's commune pays it, and pays it at par when the books work.
- **The efficiency case.** Cooking, child care and shared equipment really do have economies of scale, and co-housing exists.

## Open questions

- **Leaving the commune.** Work-units can't be saved. What happens to a member's unconverted units when she leaves, especially if the rate is below par at the time?
- **Who classifies the work?** Whether care counts as "basic" (paid from the collective fund) or "non-basic" (sold to members) is the commune's decision. TNS don't classify child care.
- **Communes with many dependants.** Should their care be society-wide, as the simulation decided for child care and disabled care, even when it happens inside a commune?
- **Is a commune without its own unit still a commune?** In GIC terms it is a residence, a consumers' cooperative and a cluster of operational units. Whether that keeps enough of what TNS wanted (community, democracy) is a question for Patrick.
